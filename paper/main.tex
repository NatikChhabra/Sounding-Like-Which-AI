\begin{abstract}
Much of today's writing is said to ``sound like AI''. Prior work measures how much text was written or revised
by large language models (LLMs); we ask a different question: how much \emph{human} writing occupies the same
measurable stylistic region as LLM output, regardless of who wrote it, and how much of that overlap predates
LLMs. We define \textbf{Machine-Style Overlap} (\mso{}), the share of a genre's human-written documents whose
topic-robust style features are at least as typical of LLM output as of pre-LLM human writing, and show that
its pre-LLM value is the human half of a Bayes error rather than detector noise. We estimate \mso{} in
argumentative student essays, arXiv abstracts (2015--2026) and Australian parliamentary speech, using text from
five local open-weight models and four commercial systems. \TODO{headline arXiv baseline and post-2022 excess}.
In student essays, \R{essay_mso_by_group.all.mso}\% of pre-LLM essays already fall in the LLM region; essays by
English-language learners are \emph{less} likely to do so than essays by their peers (adjusted odds ratio
\R{essay_ell_adjusted.odds_ratio}), the opposite of the bias reported for perplexity-based detectors. Whether
human text ``sounds like AI'' therefore depends on the instrument asking. This paper was written by an AI
system and verified by its human author; we document that process in full.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Two observations about writing after November 2022 sit uneasily together. On one side, large corpus studies
find clear traces of LLM-assisted writing in scientific abstracts, peer reviews, press releases and
encyclopedia articles \citep{kobak2025, liang2024mapping, liang2024monitoring, liang2025widespread, brooks2024},
and people have begun to use LLM-favoured words in unscripted speech \citep{yakura2024, anderson2025spoken}.
On the other side, readers are poor at telling human from machine text \citep{clark2021, jakesch2023, casal2023},
automated detectors can be evaded \citep{sadasivan2023, krishna2023, dugan2024raid}, and detectors flag
human writing by non-native speakers as AI-generated at high rates \citep{liang2023detectors, park2026}.
Together these findings suggest that the boundary between human and machine writing is thin, but they do not
say how thin, for which kinds of writing, or since when.

Existing measurements answer a \emph{prevalence} question: what fraction of a corpus was produced or modified
by an LLM? This paper answers an \emph{overlap} question: what fraction of human-written text is stylistically
indistinguishable from LLM output? The two differ in an informative place. Several prevalence studies report a
small but non-zero ``LLM share'' before ChatGPT existed and treat it as estimator error to be subtracted
\citep{liang2024mapping, lin2025, brooks2024}. We argue that this pre-LLM share is not noise. Under a
fixed set of style features it is a property of the two text populations: the part of human writing that
already sounded like AI before AI could write.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item We define \textbf{Machine-Style Overlap} (\mso{}) with a decision-theoretic interpretation: for pre-LLM
text, it is the human-side half of the Bayes error of separating human from LLM style (\S\ref{sec:mso}).
\item We estimate \mso{} with topic-robust stylometric features in three genres, using text from nine LLM
families and three prompting styles, with two classifier families and leave-one-model-out regions
(\S\ref{sec:data}--\ref{sec:method}).
\item We test whether arXiv abstracts moved into the LLM region after November 2022 beyond their pre-2022 trend,
against placebo break dates, a field-level dose contrast and a parliamentary-speech control
(\S\ref{sec:results}). \TODO{one-line result}
\item We show that the population most often falsely flagged by perplexity-based detectors, English-language
learners, is the \emph{least} machine-like under style-based overlap, which locates the reported bias in the
instrument rather than in the writing.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Prevalence of LLM-assisted writing.}
Excess-vocabulary methods compare observed word frequencies with a counterfactual extrapolated from pre-LLM
years \citep{kobak2025, holzwarth2026, lee2026}; distributional methods fit a mixture of human and LLM word
distributions \citep{liang2024mapping, liang2024monitoring, liang2025widespread, geng2024}; classifier- and
detector-based studies label individual documents \citep{lin2025, brooks2024, sun2025}. All target the share
of text produced with LLM help. The closest design to ours is \citet{bentley2026}, who build an LLM-associated
style index for arXiv abstracts since 2010 from a small set of markers and test for a break after 2023; and
\citet{chang2026}, who analyse a decade of convergence between human writing and machine patterns. We differ in
the estimand: a distributional overlap in a multi-feature style space whose pre-LLM level is itself the object
of study. Marker words are a moving target, since authors react to publicised markers \citep{geng2025}; we
therefore exclude content words from the region definition entirely.

\paragraph{Homogenization and human adoption.}
Writing with instruction-tuned models reduces content diversity \citep{padmakumar2024, doshi2024,
anderson2024homog}; LLM rewriting compresses stylistic variance \citep{sourati2025}; preference tuning reduces
output diversity \citep{kirk2024, wu2025monoculture} and is a plausible source of LLM-overused words
\citep{juzek2025}. Humans adopt LLM vocabulary in speech \citep{yakura2024, anderson2025spoken} and in academic
writing \citep{geng2025, lin2025}. If LLM style partly reflects preferences human raters already held, some
pre-LLM human writing should already sit inside the LLM region, which is what \mso{} measures.

\paragraph{Stylistic differences between humans and LLMs.}
Instruction-tuned LLMs differ systematically from humans on lexico-grammatical features \citep{reinhart2025,
munozortiz2024}, but genre explains more stylistic variance than source \citep{rallapalli2026}, so any overlap
measure must be genre-specific. Distribution-level comparisons such as MAUVE \citep{pillutla2021} and coverage
frameworks \citep{yang2026coverage} evaluate models against human text; we reverse the direction and measure
where human documents fall relative to an LLM region, over time.

\paragraph{Detection and perception.}
Untrained readers judge GPT-3 text at chance \citep{clark2021}; people rely on flawed cues
\citep{jakesch2023}; linguists and scientific reviewers misclassify abstracts \citep{casal2023, gao2023}.
Detectors are evadable \citep{sadasivan2023, krishna2023, dugan2024raid} and their false positives fall
disproportionately on non-native writing \citep{liang2023detectors, park2026}. We use these published findings
for claims about human perception; this study collects no human judgements.

\section{Machine-Style Overlap}
\label{sec:mso}

Fix a genre $G$ and a feature map $\phi$ from documents to style vectors. Let $H_0$ be the distribution of
$\phi(d)$ for human documents of genre $G$ written before LLM writing assistance was widely available (here,
before 30 November 2022), and $L$ the distribution of $\phi(d)$ for LLM output in the same genre, a mixture
over models and prompting styles with equal weights. For any population $S$ of human documents,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSO}_G(S) = \Pr_{d\sim S}\big[\, p_L(\phi(d)) \ge p_{H_0}(\phi(d)) \,\big].
\end{equation}
A document is \emph{in the LLM region} if its style is at least as likely under LLM output as under pre-LLM
human writing. With equal class priors, the Bayes posterior is
$\eta(x) = p_L(x)/(p_L(x)+p_{H_0}(x))$ and $\mathrm{MSO}_G(S) = \Pr_S[\eta(\phi(d)) \ge 1/2]$.

\paragraph{Pre-LLM overlap is a population property.}
For $S = H_0$, \mso{} is the false-positive rate of the Bayes-optimal classifier, and the Bayes error is
$\tfrac12\big(\mathrm{MSO}_G(H_0) + \Pr_L[\eta < \tfrac12]\big)$. The baseline is therefore the human half of an
irreducible error under $\phi$: it is zero only if human and LLM styles are perfectly separable, and it rises
towards $\tfrac12$ as the two distributions coincide. Prevalence studies that subtract the pre-period share as
``misclassification'' remove exactly this quantity.

\paragraph{Overlap after 2022.}
For later human populations $S_t$, $\mathrm{MSO}_G(S_t)$ measures how much of the genre's writing at time $t$
falls in a region defined relative to \emph{pre-LLM} human writing. It is agnostic to authorship by design:
an increase is consistent with undisclosed LLM use, with humans adopting LLM style, or with both, and we do not
attempt to separate them.

\paragraph{Estimation.}
We estimate $\eta$ with calibrated classifiers trained on balanced samples of pre-LLM human and LLM documents.
A finite-sample classifier is not Bayes-optimal, so we report two model families and leave-one-LLM-out
variants and treat their spread as part of the uncertainty.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Human text.}
\emph{Essays}: PERSUADE~2.0 \citep{crossley2024persuade}, argumentative essays by US students in grades 6--12 on
15 prompts, written before 2020, with English-language-learner (ELL) status for each writer.
\emph{arXiv abstracts}: a stratified random sample drawn through the arXiv API: for each of 8 fields (by
primary category) and each month from January 2015 to September 2026, all papers in one randomly placed window
of consecutive submission days, with window length scaled to field size; \TODO{N} abstracts after filtering.
\emph{Parliamentary speech}: the Australian Hansard database \citep{katz2025hansard}, restricted to spoken
speeches (excluding interjections and written questions), up to 150 randomly sampled per month from January
2015 to July 2025. Spoken parliamentary speech is our less-exposed control genre.
In every genre, half of the pre-cutoff documents (the \emph{train pool}) are used to train the classifier and
to seed LLM prompts; \mso{} is measured only on the remaining documents.

\paragraph{LLM text.}
We generated text locally with five open-weight models through Ollama (v0.40.3): Llama~3.1~8B, Qwen3~8B,
Gemma~3~12B, Mistral~7B and gpt-oss~20B, at temperature 0.8 with fixed seeds. Each model received three prompt
styles per genre: \emph{P1} write the text from a title or topic; \emph{P2} improve the wording of a human
draft (LLM-assisted revision); \emph{P3} write the text and ``avoid typical AI phrasing'', the instruction a
writer evading detection would give. Prompts were seeded only from train-pool documents, 150 per model and
prompt. For abstracts we add the unattacked chat-model generations in RAID \citep{dugan2024raid}, covering
GPT-4, ChatGPT and instruction-tuned Cohere, Llama, Mistral and MPT models.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Cleaning.}
All texts, human and LLM alike, are normalised identically: chat wrappers (``Here's the essay:''), markdown,
headings and trailing notes are removed; curly quotes, non-breaking hyphens and non-breaking spaces are
mapped to ASCII; every text is truncated to its first 250 words. Without this step, formatting artefacts alone
separate LLM output from human text.

\paragraph{Features.}
Features are deliberately topic-robust: rates per token of closed-class words (adpositions, auxiliaries,
conjunctions, determiners, particles, pronouns), coarse part-of-speech trigrams, dependency labels and
punctuation marks, plus mean and standard deviation of sentence length, mean word length and MTLD lexical
diversity \citep{mccarthy2010}, using spaCy 3.8 (\texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm}). No content words are used.
Features present in fewer than 1\% of training documents are dropped.

\paragraph{Classifiers.}
The primary instrument is L2-regularised logistic regression ($C{=}0.1$) on standardised features with
isotonic calibration (5-fold); the second is gradient-boosted trees, calibrated the same way. Classes are
balanced by subsampling, so a calibrated probability of $0.5$ corresponds to a likelihood ratio of one.
Cross-validation is grouped so that a revised text and its human source never fall in different folds. We
report AUC by model and prompt, \mso{} with 2{,}000-sample bootstrap intervals, recall on held-out models, and
agreement between the two instruments.

\paragraph{Time series.}
For arXiv and Hansard we compute monthly \mso{} and fit a segmented regression with a level shift and a slope
change at December 2022, weighted by monthly sample size, with Newey--West errors (6 lags). The pre-registered
quantity is the \emph{excess}, the gap between the fitted post-2022 series and the extrapolated pre-2022 trend,
evaluated at June 2025 (the last month covered by both genres). Placebo breaks are placed at November 2019 and
November 2020 within the pre-LLM data.

\subsection{Learners are the least machine-like writers (H5)}
Essays by English-language learners are less likely to fall in the LLM region than essays by their peers:
\R{essay_mso_by_group.ELL.mso}\% versus \R{essay_mso_by_group.non-ELL.mso}\%. Adjusting for essay prompt and

\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_lo}--\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_hi}, $p=\R{essay_ell_adjusted.p_value}$).
Perplexity-based detectors flag non-native writing because it is predictable \citep{liang2023detectors}; the
style region is defined by richer vocabulary, longer words and more uniform sentences, which learners use less.
The same text can thus be ``AI-like'' to one instrument and ``human-like'' to another.

\subsection{Overlap is genre-specific}
Applied to essays outside its training genre (the public human essays of \citealp{liang2023detectors}),
the essay instrument places \R{essay_liang.Real TOEFL.mso}\% of TOEFL essays and
\R{essay_liang.Real College Essays.mso}\% of US college essays in the region, close to chance, which is why
\mso{} must be estimated within a genre.

\subsection{Instrument agreement}
The two classifier families agree closely on overall \mso{} (\R{essay_instrument.mso_logreg}\% vs
\R{essay_instrument.mso_gbm}\%) and their document scores are correlated (Spearman
\R{essay_instrument.spearman_scores_logreg_vs_gbm}), but they agree only moderately on \emph{which} documents
cross the threshold (Cohen's $\kappa=\R{essay_instrument.kappa_logreg_vs_gbm}$). Population-level \mso{} is
therefore more stable than any single document's label, a point that bears directly on the use of detectors to
judge individual writers.

\section*{Limitations}
\mso{} depends on the feature map: a document can be inside the region under style features and outside it
under perplexity, as the learner result shows. Our generators are mostly small open-weight models run locally;
commercial models enter only through RAID abstracts. Post-2022 ``human'' documents may themselves be partly
LLM-written, so \mso{} cannot separate LLM use from human adoption of LLM style, and no causal claim is made.
The arXiv sample is a stratified random sample, not the full archive. PERSUADE essays predate LLMs, so the
learner analysis is cross-sectional. Australian parliamentary speech is less exposed to LLM writing but not
unexposed: speeches can be drafted with LLM help.
