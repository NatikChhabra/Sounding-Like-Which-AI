\title{Sounding Like Which AI? Machine-Style Overlap of arXiv Abstracts\\with Language Models of Different Vintages}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Studies of LLM-assisted writing estimate how much text was produced with LLM help. We ask how much writing,
whoever wrote it, falls on the LLM side of a stylistic boundary, and which LLMs define that side. We define
\textbf{Machine-Style Overlap} (\mso{}): the share of documents that a calibrated classifier on content-word-free
style features judges at least as likely to be LLM output as pre-ChatGPT human writing. In
\R{corpus_stats.arxiv_n_docs} arXiv abstracts (2015--2026), \mso{} was \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\%
before ChatGPT and by June 2025 stood \R{arxiv_its.all.excess} percentage points above its
pre-ChatGPT trend, far beyond placebo and instrument-drift gaps. Because recent LLMs are trained on recent
writing, we compare reference generators by vintage. Against generators whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT,
the gap is smaller but clear (\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.gap} points). Against one generator at a time,
overlap rises and then turns down, and the later a generator was released, the later the turn (rank correlation
\R{arxiv_vintage_peak.rho} across \R{arxiv_vintage_peak.n} generators); under the newest, including open models run
with logged decoding, it was still rising in 2026. A reference set that is not refreshed, like a fixed list of
marker words such as \emph{delve}, will eventually show the shift receding. In student essays, English
learners' writing is less machine-like than their peers', even under a GPT-2 perplexity instrument that reproduces
a published bias against TOEFL essays.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Corpus studies find traces of LLM-assisted writing in abstracts, peer reviews, press releases and
encyclopedias \citep{kobak2025, liang2024mapping, liang2024monitoring, liang2025widespread,
brooks2024}, and LLM-associated words have become more frequent in unscripted speech \citep{yakura2024,
anderson2025spoken}. Individual documents are harder: readers are poor at telling human from machine text
\citep{clark2021, jakesch2023, casal2023}, detectors can be evaded \citep{sadasivan2025, krishna2023,
dugan2024raid}, and some detectors have flagged non-native writers' human text as AI-generated at high rates
\citep{liang2023detectors}.

Existing measurements answer a \emph{prevalence} question: what fraction of a corpus was produced or modified
with an LLM? We answer an \emph{overlap} question: what fraction of a genre's writing is stylistically closer to
LLM output than to pre-ChatGPT human writing, whoever wrote it? The two questions treat one number differently.
Prevalence estimators report a small ``LLM share'' for text written before ChatGPT, which studies treat
as consistent with false positives \citep{liang2024mapping}, subtract as baseline misclassification
\citep{lin2025}, or use to calibrate thresholds \citep{brooks2024}. For a fixed feature map and reference set,
we treat that share as a property of the two text distributions and study how it changes over time.

The overlap question has a complication that prevalence studies share but rarely face directly: ``LLM
style'' is defined by whichever models are used as the reference. Recent models are trained on recent text, so
an overlap with them that rises over time may mean that writing moved toward the models or that the models
learned from the writing. We therefore compare reference generators by \emph{vintage} on a fixed task: models
whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT, models released in 2023--2025, and models released in 2026.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1)~We define Machine-Style Overlap (\mso{}), a classify-and-count quantity \citep{forman2008} that, for an
optimal classifier, equals the human-side component of the Bayes error before ChatGPT; we estimate it with
calibrated classifiers and report how the estimate varies across them (\S\ref{sec:mso}).
(2)~We measure it in arXiv abstracts against 19 reference generators released from 2023 to 2026, including four
whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT and four released in 2026 (\S\ref{sec:data}--\ref{sec:method}).
(3)~Abstracts moved far into the LLM region after 2022, more in computer science than in mathematics, with gaps
at placebo dates and under instruments trained on earlier years about a quarter as large or smaller
(\S\ref{sec:shift}).
(4)~The shift is present, though smaller, against generators that cannot have learned it from post-ChatGPT
writing; overlap with each generator peaks later the more recent the generator, so the trajectory depends on the
vintage of the reference (\S\ref{sec:vintage}).
(5)~Secondary results: marker words mined from each generator rise with its style region until 2025, and most
fall in 2026 (\S\ref{sec:markers}); we detect no change in the stylistic dispersion of abstracts beyond its pre-ChatGPT trend once length
is held fixed (\S\ref{sec:dispersion}); parliamentary speech changed little but fails its own placebo tests
(\S\ref{sec:hansard}); and, contrary to our analysis plan, essays by English-language learners are \emph{less}
machine-like than their peers' (\S\ref{sec:learners}).
Our analysis plan, with five hypotheses (H1: pre-ChatGPT \mso{} is non-zero and differs by field; H2: arXiv
\mso{} rises above its pre-ChatGPT trend; H3: more than in a less-exposed genre; H4: more in computer science
than in mathematics; H5: learner essays have higher \mso{}), was committed to version control before any
result was computed (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}). The vintage analysis was added after simulated review.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Prevalence of LLM-assisted writing.}
Excess-vocabulary methods compare word frequencies with a counterfactual extrapolated from pre-LLM years
\citep{kobak2025, holzwarth2026, lee2026}; distributional methods fit a mixture of human and LLM word
distributions \citep{liang2024mapping, liang2024monitoring, liang2025widespread, geng2024}; classifier- and
detector-based studies label documents \citep{lin2025, brooks2024, sun2025, khan2026}. All target the share of
text produced with LLM help. Closest to ours, \citet{bentley2026} track a composite LLM-associated style index alongside
vocabulary-complexity measures in arXiv abstracts, and \citet{askitas2025} finds an abrupt stylometric shift in economics
working-paper abstracts in 2023 with an event-study design and a classifier. \citet{chang2026} show that
PubMed, Wikipedia and Stack Exchange text became more predictable to small language models over 2014--2024, a
trend that began before generative AI; our pre-ChatGPT slope points the same way. We differ in the estimand, a
thresholded overlap (the flag rate of a calibrated classifier) whose pre-ChatGPT level is itself of interest; in
excluding content words from the features, since authors react to publicised markers \citep{geng2025}; and in
comparing reference generators by training-data vintage. LLM adoption also changes how much scientists write
\citep{kusumegi2025}.

\paragraph{Homogenization and human adoption.}
Writing or ideating with instruction-tuned models reduces content diversity across users \citep{padmakumar2024,
doshi2024, anderson2024homog}; LLM rewriting compresses stylistic variance \citep{sourati2025}; preference tuning
reduces output diversity \citep{kirk2024, wu2025monoculture} and plausibly produces LLM-overused words
\citep{juzek2025}. LLM-associated vocabulary has grown in human speech \citep{yakura2024, anderson2025spoken},
academic authors adapt their LLM use once overused words are publicised \citep{geng2025}, and adoption of LLM
revision varies across disciplines and author groups \citep{lin2025}. Models trained on their own outputs
degrade when synthetic data replace real data \citep{shumailov2024} but not when the two accumulate
\citep{gerstgrasser2024}. Our vintage comparison asks whether human text still provides an anchor independent
of LLM style.

\paragraph{Style of human and LLM text.}
LLM text differs systematically from human text in lexico-grammatical features \citep{reinhart2025,
munozortiz2024, berbersardinha2024, herbold2023} and in recurring part-of-speech templates \citep{shaib2024},
but texts cluster by genre more than by source \citep{rallapalli2026}, so any overlap measure must be
genre-specific. Distribution-level comparisons \citep{pillutla2021, yang2026coverage} evaluate models against
human text; we track where human documents fall relative to an LLM region.

\paragraph{Detection and perception.}
Readers misjudge machine text \citep{clark2021, jakesch2023, casal2023, gao2023}. Zero-shot detectors use
token-likelihood statistics \citep{hans2024binoculars, bao2024fastdetectgpt}, need more text as machine and human
distributions converge \citep{chakraborty2024}, and have flagged non-native writing \citep{liang2023detectors},
although detectors trained on a large writing assessment showed no such bias \citep{jiang2024} and false-positive
rates on non-native manuscripts vary widely \citep{park2026}. Detectors transfer unevenly to unseen generators
\citep{pu2023zeroshot, li2024mage}, and individual models have distinctive, identifiable output patterns
\citep{sun2025idiosyncrasies}, so a style region depends on the models that define it. We collect no human judgements.

\section{Machine-Style Overlap}
\label{sec:mso}

Fix a genre and a feature map $\phi$ from documents to style vectors. Let $P_{\text{pre}}$ be the distribution of
$\phi(d)$ for human documents written before ChatGPT's release (30 November 2022; ``pre-ChatGPT'' throughout),
and $P_{\text{LLM}}$ the distribution for LLM output in the same genre, an equal-weight mixture over a reference
set of generators and prompting styles chosen by us. For any population $S$ of documents,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSO}(S) = \Pr_{d\sim S}\big[\, p_{\text{LLM}}(\phi(d)) \ge p_{\text{pre}}(\phi(d)) \,\big].
\end{equation}
With equal priors the Bayes posterior is $\eta(x) = p_{\text{LLM}}(x)/(p_{\text{LLM}}(x)+p_{\text{pre}}(x))$, so
$\mathrm{MSO}(S) = \Pr_S[\eta(\phi(d)) \ge \tfrac12]$; we call $\{x: \eta(x) \ge \tfrac12\}$ the \emph{LLM
region}. \mso{} is the flag rate of the Bayes-optimal classifier, that is, classify-and-count
\citep{forman2008} with a calibrated threshold.

\paragraph{Pre-ChatGPT overlap.}
For $S = P_{\text{pre}}$, \mso{} is the Bayes classifier's false-positive rate, and the Bayes error is
$\tfrac12\big(\mathrm{MSO}(P_{\text{pre}}) + \Pr_{\text{LLM}}[\eta < \tfrac12]\big)$, which is zero only when the
two styles are separable and reaches $\tfrac12$ when they coincide. Prevalence studies that subtract a
pre-period share as misclassification remove the analogue of this quantity under their own feature map.

\paragraph{Overlap after ChatGPT.}
For later populations $S_t$, $\mathrm{MSO}(S_t)$ measures how much writing at time $t$ falls in the region. It
is agnostic to authorship: a rise is consistent with undisclosed LLM use, with humans adopting LLM style, or both.
If a fraction $\alpha$ of documents were LLM output and the rest unchanged human writing,
$\mathrm{MSO}(S_t) = \mathrm{FPR} + \alpha(\mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR})$; we use this identity only to translate
\mso{} into an implied $\alpha$ for comparison with prevalence studies.

\paragraph{Estimation.}
We call a trained, calibrated classifier with its 0.5 threshold an \emph{instrument}. A finite instrument only
approximates $\eta$, and its flag rate is neither an upper nor a lower bound on the Bayes quantity. We therefore
report how \mso{} varies across classifier families, held-out generators, generator sets and thresholds;
confidence intervals in the text are document-level bootstrap or regression intervals and exclude this
instrument variation.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{arXiv abstracts.}
For each of eight arXiv archives (cs, math, stat, eess, q-bio, econ, astro-ph, cond-mat; by primary
category; we use ``field'' for archive below) and each month from January 2015 to September 2026, we fetched all
papers submitted in one randomly placed window of consecutive days through the arXiv API and drew up to 200 at
random. Field-months with at least 200 papers therefore carry equal weight; smaller fields (economics in
particular) contribute fewer documents, more so in early years, so the overall series is an average over a stratified
sample rather than over the arXiv population. The API returns the latest version of each
abstract, so papers first submitted before ChatGPT but revised after it are excluded
(\R{robustness_v4.revision_exclusion_share.y2016}\% of sampled 2016 papers,
\R{robustness_v4.revision_exclusion_share.y2021}\% of 2021 and \R{robustness_v4.revision_exclusion_share.y2022}\% of 2022
papers). Recent papers have had less time to be revised, but restricting the scored abstracts to never-revised papers
leaves yearly \mso{} unchanged (2025: \R{robustness_v5.never_revised.mso_never_y2025}\% vs
\R{robustness_v5.never_revised.mso_all_y2025}\%; 2026: \R{robustness_v5.never_revised.mso_never_y2026}\% vs
\R{robustness_v5.never_revised.mso_all_y2026}\%). After removing abstracts under 50 words,
\R{corpus_stats.arxiv_n_docs} abstracts remain.

\paragraph{Other human text.}
\emph{Parliamentary speech:} the Australian House of Representatives Hansard \citep{katz2023scidata,
katz2025hansard}, spoken speeches only, up to 150 per month from January 2015 to July 2025
(\R{corpus_stats.hansard_n_docs} speeches), a lightly edited transcript that we treat as less exposed to LLM
writing. \emph{Student essays:} PERSUADE~2.0 \citep{crossley2024persuade}, \R{corpus_stats.essay_n_docs}
argumentative essays by US students in grades 6--12 on \R{essay_ell_counts.n_prompts} prompts, written before
ChatGPT, with English-language-learner (ELL) status and a holistic score for each essay; these are school
learners, a different population from the TOEFL test-takers of \citet{liang2023detectors}.
In every genre half of the pre-ChatGPT documents (the \emph{train pool}) train the instrument and seed LLM
prompts; \mso{} is measured only on the remaining documents.

\paragraph{Reference generators.}
Table~\ref{tab:gens} lists them. Local models answered three prompt styles, 150 prompts each:
\emph{P1} write the text from a title or topic; \emph{P2} improve the wording of a human draft (LLM-assisted
revision); \emph{P3} write the text and ``avoid typical AI phrasing''. Prompts were seeded only from train-pool
documents; local models used temperature 0.8 and fixed seeds. The 2026 model (Claude Opus~5.5) answered the same
prompt instances through sub-agent sessions of 50 prompts each, without control over decoding; it is also the
system that drafted this paper (see AI Use). For abstracts we add the RAID generations without adversarial attack

14 generators, each capped at 450 training texts. Five further open models were added after review for the
vintage analysis only: two whose pretraining
data predate ChatGPT and three released in 2026, which give the 2026 comparison a second source with logged
decoding and pair with 2025 models of the same families. Hansard and essays use the seven local models and the
2026 model.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Cleaning.}
All texts pass through the same normalisation: chat wrappers (``Here's the abstract:''), lines echoing the
prompt's title, markdown and trailing notes are removed; typographic variants and mis-decoded characters are
mapped to plain equivalents; LaTeX math becomes a placeholder and LaTeX dashes (\texttt{-{}-}, \texttt{-{}-{}-})
become Unicode dashes, since human arXiv abstracts write them in LaTeX while chat models emit Unicode, which would
otherwise separate the sources by encoding alone. Texts are truncated to 250 words.

\paragraph{Features and instruments.}
Features exclude content words to limit topic sensitivity: per-token rates of closed-class words, coarse
part-of-speech trigrams, dependency labels and punctuation marks, plus mean and standard deviation of sentence
length, mean word length and MTLD lexical diversity \citep{mccarthy2010}, computed with spaCy~3.8
(\texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm}); features present in fewer than 1\% of training documents are dropped
(top features in Appendix~\ref{app:features}). The primary instrument is L2-regularised logistic regression
($C{=}0.1$) on standardised features with isotonic calibration; the second uses gradient-boosted trees,
calibrated the same way. Classes are balanced, so a calibrated probability of 0.5 is a likelihood ratio of one.
Cross-validation and calibration folds are grouped so that a revised text and its human source, and all speeches
by one parliamentarian, never fall in different folds. For essays we add two instruments: logistic regression on
sentence embeddings (all-MiniLM-L6-v2), and a one-feature isotonic classifier on the mean token negative
log-likelihood of the essay under a small causal language model (Qwen2.5-0.5B, and GPT-2 small, whose training
data predate the TOEFL and PERSUADE releases), the statistic behind perplexity-based detectors. Both are trained on the
same human and LLM essays as the primary instrument.

\paragraph{Time series.}
We compute monthly \mso{} and fit an interrupted time-series model: weighted least squares with a level shift and
a slope change at December 2022 and Newey--West (6 lags) standard errors. The \emph{trend gap} is the fitted
post-ChatGPT value minus the extrapolated pre-ChatGPT trend in June 2025, a month fixed in code before any
time-series result. As robustness checks we vary the pre-trend (quadratic; start in 2017 or 2019; end in 2020),
compute a nonparametric gap (mean observed January--June 2025 value minus the extrapolated trend), and bootstrap
the gap with 12-month residual blocks. Placebo breaks are placed every second month from June 2016 to December
2021, each evaluated 12 months later. Drift placebos train the instrument on human abstracts up to 2016, 2017 or
2018 only and test for a gap in June 2022 over that trend, 41 to 65 months after the fake break and before any LLM
writing assistance, so any gap they show reflects instrument ageing.

\paragraph{Vintage.}
To separate generators by vintage at a fixed task we train one instrument per generator on its title-only (P1)
outputs (RAID outputs are title-based) and one on generators whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT (the
\emph{pre-ChatGPT-data region}): GPT-4 and Llama-2-70B-chat from RAID plus Llama-2-Chat~7B and Vicuna~7B run
locally on P1. Many RAID outputs summarise the paper (``In the paper titled\dots''); we drop those matching a
summary pattern (\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data.summary_register_share}\% of the RAID part of this set).

\paragraph{Marker words.}
We track two lexical series: the share of abstracts containing a published marker word (\emph{delve},
\emph{underscore}, \emph{showcase}, \emph{meticulous}, \emph{intricate}, \emph{intricacies},
\emph{commendable}, \emph{boast}; \citealp{kobak2025, gray2024, yakura2024}), and, for each reference set, the
share containing one of 20 words mined from that set's own outputs: the words with the highest smoothed log
ratio of document frequency between the outputs and the human abstracts their prompts were built from, so that
most topic words of the prompt set cancel.

\subsection{Separable, but not completely}
The instruments separate pre-ChatGPT human text from LLM text well (cross-validated AUC
\R{arxiv_instrument.cv_auc_all} for abstracts, \R{hansard_instrument.cv_auc_all} for speech,
\R{essay_instrument.cv_auc_all} for essays). Generators held out of training are mostly recognised
(arXiv recall from \R{arxiv_lomo.raid-cohere-chat.recall_on_unseen_model}\% for Cohere to
\R{arxiv_lomo.raid-gpt4.recall_on_unseen_model}\% for GPT-4; Table~\ref{tab:lomo}), but the 2026 model is not:
only \R{arxiv_lomo.claude-opus-5.5.recall_on_unseen_model}\% of its abstracts and
\R{essay_lomo.claude-opus-5.5.recall_on_unseen_model}\% of its essays fall in a region built without it. Its
revisions of human drafts are also hard to separate from human text (AUC
\R{arxiv_separability.claude-opus-5.5/P2.auc}). The top LLM-typical feature in abstracts is lexical diversity,
followed by determiner--noun--verb sequences, commas and \emph{that}; human abstracts use more coordination,
adjectival modifiers, \emph{by}, longer sentences and \emph{we} (Appendix~\ref{app:features}).

\subsection{Pre-ChatGPT overlap is structured (H1)}
Before ChatGPT, \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% (95\% CI \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.lo}--%
\R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.hi}) of arXiv abstracts fell in the LLM region, as did
\R{hansard_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% (\R{hansard_baseline_pre2022.all.lo}--\R{hansard_baseline_pre2022.all.hi})
of speeches and \R{essay_mso_by_group.all.mso}\% (\R{essay_mso_by_group.all.lo}--\R{essay_mso_by_group.all.hi})
of student essays. The arXiv baseline varies by field (Figure~\ref{fig:fields} in Appendix~\ref{app:fields}; $\chi^2$
$p\R{arxiv_its.baseline_field_heterogeneity.p}$), from \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.astro-ph.mso}\% in astrophysics
to \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.econ.mso}\% in economics. Fields also differ in abstract length; reweighting each
field to the overall length distribution changes the values to \R{arxiv_baseline_lenstd.astro-ph.mso_lenstd}\%
and \R{arxiv_baseline_lenstd.econ.mso_lenstd}\%, and abstracts without math give a similar ordering. Overall
\mso{} was also rising slowly before ChatGPT (\R{arxiv_its.all.pre_slope_per_year}\pp{} per year).



\subsection{Abstracts moved into the region (H2, H4)}
\label{sec:shift}
Yearly arXiv \mso{} ranged from \R{arxiv_yearly.2016.mso}\% to \R{arxiv_yearly.2020.mso}\% in 2015--2021 and was
\R{arxiv_yearly.2022.mso}\% in 2022. It was \R{arxiv_yearly.2023.mso}\%, \R{arxiv_yearly.2024.mso}\% and
\R{arxiv_yearly.2025.mso}\% in 2023--2025 and \R{arxiv_yearly.2026.mso}\% in January--September 2026; the monthly
series has been roughly flat since late 2025 (Figure~\ref{fig:ts}a). The trend gap in June 2025 is
\R{arxiv_its.all.excess}\pp{} (95\% CI \R{arxiv_its.all.excess_lo}--\R{arxiv_its.all.excess_hi}; block
bootstrap \R{robustness_v4.arxiv_block_bootstrap.lo}--\R{robustness_v4.arxiv_block_bootstrap.hi}), about
\R{robustness_v4.arxiv_pretrend.relative_gap} times the extrapolated trend value. Across the variants in
Table~\ref{tab:robust} that change the threshold, the sample or the trend, the gap stays between
\R{arxiv_its.threshold_06.excess}\pp{} (threshold 0.6) and \R{arxiv_its.no_math.excess}\pp{} (abstracts without math).
It is smaller when the generators that write closest to human abstracts are left out of the region: without the
2026 model (\R{arxiv_gensets_its.no_frontier.excess}\pp) or without revisions of human drafts
(\R{arxiv_vintage.all_no_revision.gap}\pp). No single feature family carries it: removing MTLD, the strongest
feature, gives \R{arxiv_ablation.mtld.excess}\pp, and removing all closed-class words, POS trigrams, dependency
labels, punctuation or sentence statistics gives between \R{arxiv_ablation.function_words.excess} and
\R{arxiv_ablation.pos_trigrams.excess}\pp. An off-the-shelf zero-shot statistic behaves differently: the analytic
Fast-DetectGPT criterion \citep{bao2024fastdetectgpt} computed with GPT-2 small separates our human and LLM
abstracts only moderately (AUC \R{arxiv_fastdetect_auc.heldin_auc}), and its calibrated flag rate on 600 abstracts
per year does not rise (\R{arxiv_fastdetect.2015.share_flagged}\% in 2015, \R{arxiv_fastdetect.2022.share_flagged}\% in
2022, \R{arxiv_fastdetect.2026.share_flagged}\% in 2026). A detector based on token predictability under a small
older model would miss the shift; larger scoring models, as in the original method, may not. Of \R{robustness_v3.arxiv_placebo_grid.n_breaks} placebo
breaks placed every second month from 2016 to 2021, \R{robustness_v3.arxiv_placebo_grid.share_p_below_05}\% give a
gap with $p<0.05$, close to the nominal 5\%, and the largest is \R{robustness_v3.arxiv_placebo_grid.max_abs_gap}\pp{}
(with the real gap's 30-month horizon, \R{robustness_v5.arxiv_placebo_grid_30m.share_p_below_05}\% and at most

the implied share for mathematics somewhat.

\subsection{Sounding like which AI?}
\label{sec:vintage}
\emph{Generators whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT.} The pre-ChatGPT-data region (GPT-4, Llama-2-70B-chat,
Llama-2-Chat~7B and Vicuna~7B on the title-only task) reflects models that cannot have learned their style from
post-ChatGPT scientific writing (their instruction tuning used 2023 data, but not arXiv abstracts).
Abstracts still moved into it: \mso{} was \R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.y2022}\% in 2022,
\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.y2024}\% in 2024 and \R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.y2025}\% in 2025,
and the trend gap is \R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.gap}\pp{} (CI
\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.gap_lo}--\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.gap_hi}), against
\R{arxiv_vintage.local_p1.gap}\pp{} for a title-only region built from the 2024--2025 open models
(Figure~\ref{fig:vintage}a). Variants agree: with summary-style RAID outputs kept
(\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data.gap}\pp), with ChatGPT added (\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_plus_chatgpt.gap}\pp), or
with only the two small local models, run on exactly our prompts (\R{arxiv_vintage.old_open_p1.gap}\pp, CI
\R{arxiv_vintage.old_open_p1.gap_lo}--\R{arxiv_vintage.old_open_p1.gap_hi}). Three quarters of the region's training
texts come from RAID, so the larger estimate leans on RAID's prompt. Part of the shift is therefore toward the style
of the pre-ChatGPT-data generators and is unlikely to be an artefact of newer models learning from newer abstracts,
though the controlled estimate is small. Drift placebos for this region (instruments trained on abstracts up to 2016, 2017 or 2018) give
gaps of \R{robustness_v5.pre2022data_drift_to_2016.excess}, $\R{robustness_v5.pre2022data_drift_to_2017.excess}$ and
$\R{robustness_v5.pre2022data_drift_to_2018.excess}$\pp. In 2026 its \mso{} fell back to
\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.y2026}\%.

\emph{One generator at a time.} With the region defined by a single generator on the title-only task, \mso{} rose
from 2022 to 2025 under every generator except Cohere's chat model. Followed quarter by quarter, overlap with each
generator rises and then turns down, and the turning point comes later for later generators
(Figure~\ref{fig:vintage}b): it peaked between 2024Q3 and 2025Q2 under the 2023 models, in 2025Q2--Q3 under the
2024 models, in 2025Q4 under Gemma~3, Granite~3.3 and Qwen3, and in 2026Q1--Q2 under gpt-oss, Qwen3.5 and
Granite~4.2; under the 2026 model it was still rising in 2026Q3. The rank correlation between release date and
peak quarter is \R{arxiv_vintage_peak.rho} ($n=\R{arxiv_vintage_peak.n}$, $p\R{arxiv_vintage_peak.rho_p}$;
an exploratory analysis added after review). Gemma~4 is the clearest exception: released in June 2026, it peaked in
2025Q4. On annual means, \mso{} fell from 2025 to 2026 under \R{arxiv_vintage_summary.n_fell_2026} of the
\R{arxiv_vintage_summary.n_single} generators and rose under gpt-oss
(\R{arxiv_vintage.single:gpt-oss:20b.y2025}\% to \R{arxiv_vintage.single:gpt-oss:20b.y2026}\%), Qwen3.5, Granite~4.2
and the 2026 model (\R{arxiv_vintage.single:claude-opus-5.5.y2025}\% to
\R{arxiv_vintage.single:claude-opus-5.5.y2026}\%). Each change has a document-bootstrap interval that excludes zero
and keeps its sign across five draws of the human training sample, except under Gemma~4
(\R{arxiv_vintage_uncertainty.single:gemma4:12b.change_seed0}\pp, CI
$\R{arxiv_vintage_uncertainty.single:gemma4:12b.change_lo}$ to \R{arxiv_vintage_uncertainty.single:gemma4:12b.change_hi}).
Measured from 2025Q4 instead, only gpt-oss, Granite~4.2 and the 2026 model rose and Qwen3.5 was flat
(\R{arxiv_vintage_quarterly.single:qwen3.5:9b.change_q4}\pp). Within families, the newer model fared better in all
three pairs, but only Granite shows opposite signs (from 2025Q4: Granite~3.3
$\R{arxiv_vintage_quarterly.single:granite3.3:8b.change_q4}$\pp, Granite~4.2
\R{arxiv_vintage_quarterly.single:granite4.2:8b.change_q4}\pp). Known pretraining cutoffs do not sort the generators:
gpt-oss (cutoff June 2024) peaked late while Gemma~4 (January 2025) peaked early. Release date sorts them better,
which points to the style of recent post-training rather than to the recency of pretraining data. The three 2026
open models, run with logged decoding, rose with the 2026 model (\R{arxiv_vintage.open2026_p1.y2025}\% to
\R{arxiv_vintage.open2026_p1.y2026}\% on annual means), so the late peak does not rest on the uncontrolled generator.

The aggregate 14-generator series in Figure~\ref{fig:ts}a mixes these: it still rose in 2026, carried by gpt-oss and
the 2026 model, while 12 of its 14 single-generator regions fell. Because overlap with each generator turns down
later the more recent the generator, a reference set that is not refreshed will eventually show the shift receding,
even if writing keeps moving. We cannot tell whether recent abstracts moved toward the newest models or the
newest models learned from recent writing; both produce this pattern. Of our comparisons, only the
pre-ChatGPT-data region avoids that ambiguity.

\subsection{Marker words}
\label{sec:markers}
The share of abstracts with a published marker word rose from \R{arxiv_yearly.2022.marker}\% in 2022 to
\R{arxiv_yearly.2024.marker}\% in 2024 and fell to \R{arxiv_yearly.2026.marker}\% in 2026 (Figure~\ref{fig:ts}c);
its June 2025 trend gap was \R{arxiv_its.marker.excess}\pp{} (CI \R{arxiv_its.marker.excess_lo}--%
\R{arxiv_its.marker.excess_hi}). These words come from 2023--2024 models, so we also compare with words mined
from the generators that define each style region. Words mined from each generator's own outputs
(\S\ref{sec:method}) behave like their style regions until 2025: for every generator, the share of abstracts using
one of its 20 words rose from 2022 to 2025 (for the pre-ChatGPT-data generators from
\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.m2022}\% to \R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.m2025}\%). In 2026 the share
fell for \R{arxiv_vintage_summary.n_marker_fell_2026} of the \R{arxiv_vintage_summary.n_single} generators,
including gpt-oss and Qwen3.5, under which style overlap rose; it kept rising only for Granite~4.2
(\R{arxiv_vintage.single:granite4.2:8b.m2025}\% to \R{arxiv_vintage.single:granite4.2:8b.m2026}\%) and the 2026 model
(\R{arxiv_vintage.single:claude-opus-5.5.m2025}\% to \R{arxiv_vintage.single:claude-opus-5.5.m2026}\%), whose
mined words (\emph{substantial}, \emph{comparable}, \emph{implications}, \emph{whose}) are ordinary academic
vocabulary. In 2026, then, lexical and stylistic measures diverge for some recent generators and agree for older
ones. Once markers and style region come from the same model, the decline of \emph{delve} mostly reflects which
models the markers come from. At the document level the published markers and \mso{}
pick out largely different abstracts (point-biserial $r=\R{arxiv_marker_vs_mso.corr_marker_inregion}$;
\R{arxiv_marker_vs_mso.share_inregion_with_marker}\% of in-region versus
\R{arxiv_marker_vs_mso.share_outregion_with_marker}\% of other abstracts contain one).

\subsection{Secondary results}
\label{sec:hansard}\label{sec:dispersion}\label{sec:learners}
\emph{Parliamentary speech (H3).} The Hansard trend gap is small (\R{hansard_its.all.excess}\pp, CI
$\R{hansard_its.all.excess_lo}$ to \R{hansard_its.all.excess_hi}), and the arXiv gap exceeds it by
\R{control_difference.difference}\pp{} (CI \R{control_difference.lo}--\R{control_difference.hi}) with both regions
built from the same generators. Hansard is a weak comparison: it fails its own placebo grid
(\R{robustness_v3.hansard_placebo_grid.share_p_below_05}\% of breaks with $p<0.05$) and its gap depends on the pre-trend
specification (Appendix~\ref{app:hansard}). \emph{Dispersion.} With abstracts cut to 150 words, the stylistic spread
of abstracts did not fall faster after ChatGPT than before (2025 gap \R{arxiv_len150_dispersion_trend.gap_2025.est},
CI $\R{arxiv_len150_dispersion_trend.gap_2025.ci_lo}$ to \R{arxiv_len150_dispersion_trend.gap_2025.ci_hi};
Appendix~\ref{app:dispersion}). \emph{Learner essays (H5, not supported).} Our plan predicted higher \mso{} for
English learners; it is lower (\R{essay_mso_by_group.ELL.mso}\% vs \R{essay_mso_by_group.non-ELL.mso}\%; odds ratio
\R{essay_ell_adjusted.odds_ratio}, \R{essay_ell_adjusted.odds_ratio_prof} after adjusting for proficiency), and
sentence-embedding and perplexity instruments agree in direction. A GPT-2 perplexity instrument flags
\R{essay_liang_perplexity_gpt2.Real TOEFL.mso}\% of TOEFL essays but \R{essay_liang_perplexity_gpt2.Real US 8th grade.mso}\%
of US 8th-grade essays, reproducing the bias reported by \citet{liang2023detectors}, while flagging these school
learners less than their peers: which learners such a detector penalises depends on how predictable their writing
is (Appendix~\ref{app:learners}).

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What ``sounding like AI'' measures.}
Some human writing sat in the LLM region before ChatGPT, more in some genres and fields than others; abstracts
then moved far into it, partly toward the style of the pre-ChatGPT-data generators. In
essays the region is largely one of fluent, lexically diverse writing, so there ``sounding like AI'' partly
reflects writing quality.

\paragraph{Trajectories depend on the reference.}
Whether abstracts were still moving toward LLM style in 2026 depends on which LLM: overlap with each generator
peaked later the more recent the generator, and under the newest it was still rising. Lexical markers recede earlier: words mined
from most generators, including two whose style overlap kept rising, fell in 2026, so the fall of \emph{delve} is
not on its own evidence that writing stopped moving. Detectors, prevalence estimators and marker lists built on a
fixed set of models may understate recent
change unless the reference is refreshed, and comparisons across years should report the vintage of their
reference. Our data cannot say why abstracts in 2026 resemble the newest models: authors may use those models,
or the models may have learned from recent abstracts. Under either reading, LLM style and human scientific
style are no longer independent anchors for each other. This is a precondition for the recursive dynamics
studied in model-collapse work \citep{shumailov2024, gerstgrasser2024}, though not evidence of them.

\paragraph{Implications for detection.}
Population-level \mso{} is similar across classifier families for abstracts (\R{arxiv_instrument.mso_logreg}\% vs
\R{arxiv_instrument.mso_gbm}\% of scored abstracts), but agreement on individual documents is moderate there
(Cohen's $\kappa=\R{arxiv_instrument.kappa_logreg_vs_gbm}$) and low for speech
($\kappa=\R{hansard_instrument.kappa_logreg_vs_gbm}$) and essays ($\kappa=\R{essay_instrument.kappa_logreg_vs_gbm}$).
Every instrument we tried flags non-learner essays more often than learner essays, and a perplexity instrument
reverses its bias between two learner populations. These findings argue against using any single score to judge
an individual writer.

\section{Conclusion}
Machine-Style Overlap measures how much of a genre's writing sits on the LLM side of a stylistic boundary. In
arXiv abstracts it rose from \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% before ChatGPT to \R{arxiv_yearly.2025.mso}\% in
2025, far beyond trend, partly toward the style of the pre-ChatGPT-data generators. Overlap with each generator
peaked later the more recent the generator, so measurements of LLM influence on writing should report, and
refresh, the vintage of their reference models.

\section*{Limitations}
\mso{} is defined relative to a feature map and a researcher-chosen generator set, and its trajectory after 2025
depends on that set's vintage (\S\ref{sec:vintage}). Four generators are from 2026; one of them was run without
decoding control in batches of 50 prompts and is the system that drafted this paper. The pre-ChatGPT-data region rests
on four generators, three of them built on Llama~2, and their instruction tuning used data from 2023. The vintage
comparison holds the prompt fixed but not output length (older models write shorter abstracts) or, for RAID,
decoding; the three 2026 open models were added after the pattern of the other generators had been seen, and
single-generator regions rest on 150 texts each. Mined marker lists contain some topic and register words. Post-ChatGPT
``human'' documents may be partly LLM-written, so \mso{} cannot separate LLM use from human adoption of LLM
style, and no causal claim is made. The overall arXiv series averages a stratified sample of eight fields
(two of them in physics) and is not the arXiv population; changes in authorship, subfields and submission volume may contribute despite
stratified sampling and topic and length checks, and excluding papers revised after ChatGPT makes the late
pre-period sample more selected. Confidence intervals exclude instrument variation and the field-month cluster
design. All text is English and truncated to 250 words. Parliamentary speech differs from abstracts in many ways,
its linear trend fails its own placebo tests, and the 2022 change of government altered the speaker population
six months before ChatGPT. The dispersion measure depends on document length. The learner analysis is
cross-sectional, concerns US school learners, and rests on \R{essay_ell_counts.ell_in_region} in-region learner
essays; its perplexity instruments use two small language models and thresholds calibrated on PERSUADE. The
analysis plan was committed to our own repository and never frozen with a third party.

from $\R{robustness_v4.hansard_pretrend.linear_to_2020}$ to \R{robustness_v4.hansard_pretrend.quadratic_2015}\pp{}
across pre-trend specifications, \R{robustness_v3.hansard_placebo_grid.share_p_below_05}\% of its placebo breaks
give $p<0.05$, and the 2022 change of government altered the speaker population six months before ChatGPT.
Published marker words also rose in speeches (\R{hansard_its.marker.excess}\pp{} above trend). We read Hansard as
evidence that parliamentary speech changed much less than abstracts.


\section{Dispersion}
\label{app:dispersion}
To connect overlap to homogenization we measured, for each year, the mean Euclidean distance of abstracts to the
year's centroid in the feature space standardised on pre-ChatGPT abstracts. On all abstracts, dispersion falls
steadily from \R{arxiv_dispersion.2015.dispersion} in 2015 to \R{arxiv_dispersion.2026.dispersion} in 2026, but
abstracts also grew longer, and per-token rates are less noisy in longer texts. With every abstract cut to its first
150 words (abstracts of at least 150 words, 1,200 per year), dispersion declines slowly before ChatGPT
(\R{arxiv_len150_dispersion_trend.gap_2025.trend_per_year} per year) and in 2025 lies
\R{arxiv_len150_dispersion_trend.gap_2025.est} above that trend (bootstrap CI
$\R{arxiv_len150_dispersion_trend.gap_2025.ci_lo}$ to \R{arxiv_len150_dispersion_trend.gap_2025.ci_hi}). The interval
excludes an extra decline larger than about three years of the pre-ChatGPT trend, so any faster loss of stylistic
spread is small next to the shift toward the LLM region; diversity of content or ideas may behave differently \citep{padmakumar2024, doshi2024}.


\section{Learner essays}
\label{app:learners}
Our analysis plan predicted that essays by English-language learners would show \emph{higher} \mso{}. They show
lower: \R{essay_mso_by_group.ELL.mso}\% versus \R{essay_mso_by_group.non-ELL.mso}\%
(\R{essay_ell_counts.ell_in_region} of \R{essay_ell_counts.n_ell} learner essays in the region; odds ratio
adjusted for prompt and grade \R{essay_ell_adjusted.odds_ratio}, CI
\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_lo}--\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_hi}; lower, and significant at $p<0.05$, in all
\R{essay_seed_stability.n_seeds} training draws). Most of the difference is proficiency: adding the holistic
essay score reduces the odds ratio to \R{essay_ell_adjusted.odds_ratio_prof} (CI
\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_lo_prof}--\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_hi_prof}) and the continuous classifier margin from
$\R{essay_ell_continuous.ell_coef_sd}$ to $\R{essay_ell_continuous.ell_coef_sd_prof}$ standard deviations (CI
$\R{essay_ell_continuous.coef_lo_prof}$ to $\R{essay_ell_continuous.coef_hi_prof}$). In essays the LLM region is
largely a region of fluent, lexically diverse writing. The other instruments agree in direction
(Figure~\ref{fig:learners}): sentence embeddings (\R{essay_embedding_instrument.ELL.mso}\% vs
\R{essay_embedding_instrument.non-ELL.mso}\%) and perplexity under GPT-2 (separating human from LLM essays with
AUC \R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.heldin_auc}, against \R{essay_instrument.cv_auc_all} for style features, so it
flags \R{essay_perplexity_instrument_gpt2.all.mso}\% of all human essays;
\R{essay_perplexity_instrument_gpt2.ELL.mso}\% vs \R{essay_perplexity_instrument_gpt2.non-ELL.mso}\%; odds ratio
\R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.odds_ratio}, and \R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.odds_ratio_prof}, CI
\R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.or_lo_prof}--\R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.or_hi_prof}, after adjusting
for proficiency), because these learners' essays are \emph{less} predictable (mean token log-perplexity
\R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.mean_logppl_ell} vs \R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.mean_logppl_nonell}).
The same GPT-2 instrument reproduces the bias reported by \citet{liang2023detectors} on their data: it flags
\R{essay_liang_perplexity_gpt2.Real TOEFL.mso}\% of TOEFL essays by non-native test-takers, which are highly
predictable (\R{essay_liang_perplexity_gpt2.Real TOEFL.mean_logppl}), but
\R{essay_liang_perplexity_gpt2.Real US 8th grade.mso}\% of US 8th-grade essays
(\R{essay_liang_perplexity_gpt2.Real US 8th grade.mean_logppl}); Qwen2.5-0.5B gives the same pattern
(\R{essay_liang_perplexity.Real TOEFL.mso}\% vs \R{essay_liang_perplexity.Real US 8th grade.mso}\%). GPT-2's training data predate the public release of the TOEFL essays (and PERSUADE), so that contrast
does not come from memorised test essays; the 8th-grade essays derive from data public since 2012. In these data
a perplexity instrument penalises predictable writing: the TOEFL essays are predictable and these school
learners' essays are not, so which learners such a detector flags depends on the population, consistent with
detectors that showed no bias in a large writing assessment \citep{jiang2024}. The TOEFL and 8th-grade sets also
differ in genre, prompt, age and length.

\section{Deviations from the analysis plan}
\label{app:deviations}
The plan was committed to version control before any result was computed (the commit will be
identified after review); it was never frozen with a third party. (1)~arXiv was sampled through the API with a stratified design instead of full harvesting; the first
sampler truncated large windows and was replaced (only the second is used); pre-ChatGPT papers revised after
ChatGPT are excluded. (2)~PERSUADE~2.0 replaced ELLIPSE, which could
not be obtained; the essays of \citet{liang2023detectors} (TOEFL, US 8th-grade and US college) are a different
genre and are used only for the perplexity replication. The style instrument places
\R{essay_liang.Real TOEFL.mso}\% of TOEFL, \R{essay_liang.Real US 8th grade.mso}\% of 8th-grade and
\R{essay_liang.Real College Essays.mso}\% of college essays in the PERSUADE-defined region, which shows how
genre-specific the region is. (3)~Decided after an arXiv pilot on partial data and before the first review:
Phi-4, Granite and the 2026 model. The 2026 model's texts were merged two minutes after a preliminary arXiv run
without them was committed; Phi-4 and Granite were first used after the first review. The pilot may have informed
this decision. (4)~Added after the first review: LaTeX and encoding
normalisation, grouped calibration, generator-set, drift, math, topic, length and dash analyses, the proficiency
adjustment, the perplexity instrument and the dispersion measure. (5)~Added after the second review: the vintage
analysis with Llama-2-Chat~7B and Vicuna~7B; the three 2026 open models (Qwen3.5, Gemma~4, Granite~4.2), added
after the single-generator pattern of the other 16 generators had been seen; mined marker words, the placebo grid, pre-trend variants, block
bootstrap, further drift origins, Holm correction and the cs--mathematics contrast, length-fixed dispersion and
GPT-2 perplexity; two cleaning fixes (whole ``Title:'' lines, keyword lines, word-count notes and inline labels in LLM outputs are now
dropped), after each of which every analysis was re-run. (5b)~Added after the third review: quarterly
single-generator series, seed ranges and intervals for the 2026 changes, drift placebos for the pre-ChatGPT-data
region, a 30-month placebo grid, a never-revised check, feature-family ablations and a zero-shot detector baseline. (6)~The self-test of the paper's own text and the plan's arXiv embedding instrument were
dropped. (7)~The plan named cs.CL for one comparison; all of cs is used. The June 2025 evaluation month was fixed
in code, not in the plan. Hansard was restricted to 2015--2025. H5 was not supported.
