\title{Sounding Like AI Before AI: Machine-Style Overlap in Human Writing}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Corpus studies of large language models (LLMs) ask how much text was written with LLM help. We ask how much
\emph{human} writing is stylistically indistinguishable from LLM output, regardless of who wrote it, and how
much of that overlap predates LLMs. We define \textbf{Machine-Style Overlap} (\mso{}): the share of a genre's
documents whose topic-robust style features are at least as typical of LLM output as of pre-LLM human writing.
For pre-LLM text, \mso{} is the human half of a Bayes error, so the ``false positives'' that prevalence studies
subtract are a population property. In 159k arXiv abstracts (2015--2026), \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\%
of pre-ChatGPT abstracts already sat in the LLM region, ranging from \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.astro-ph.mso}\%
in astrophysics to \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.econ.mso}\% in economics. After November 2022, \mso{} rose
\R{arxiv_its.all.excess_at_2025-06} points above its pre-2022 trend, versus \R{hansard_its.all.excess_at_2025-06}
points in parliamentary speech; the rise is largest in computer science and smallest in mathematics. Marker
words such as \emph{delve} rose and then fell back towards baseline, while \mso{} stayed high. Among
pre-LLM student essays, English-language learners are \emph{less} machine-like than their peers, the opposite
of the bias reported for perplexity-based detectors. Whether text ``sounds like AI'' depends on the instrument
asking, and for scientific prose the answer has changed more than word counts suggest.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Two findings about writing since November 2022 sit uneasily together. Large corpus studies find clear traces of
LLM-assisted writing in scientific abstracts, peer reviews, press releases and encyclopedia articles
\citep{kobak2025, liang2024mapping, liang2024monitoring, liang2025widespread, brooks2024}, and LLM-favoured
words have entered unscripted speech \citep{yakura2024, anderson2025spoken}. Yet people are poor at telling
human from machine text \citep{clark2021, jakesch2023, casal2023}, detectors can be evaded
\citep{sadasivan2023, krishna2023, dugan2024raid}, and detectors flag non-native writers' human text as
AI-generated at high rates \citep{liang2023detectors, park2026}. The boundary between human and machine writing
appears thin, but existing work does not say how thin, for which kinds of writing, or since when.

Existing measurements answer a \emph{prevalence} question: what fraction of a corpus was produced or modified
by an LLM? We answer an \emph{overlap} question: what fraction of human-written text is stylistically
indistinguishable from LLM output? The two diverge in an informative place. Several prevalence studies observe
a small but non-zero ``LLM share'' before ChatGPT existed and treat it as estimator error
\citep{liang2024mapping, lin2025, brooks2024}. We argue that this pre-LLM share is not noise. Under a fixed
feature map it is a property of the two text populations: the part of human writing that already sounded like
AI before AI could write.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1)~We define Machine-Style Overlap (\mso{}) and show that its pre-LLM value is the human half of a Bayes error
(\S\ref{sec:mso}). (2)~We estimate \mso{} in three genres with topic-robust stylometric features and text
from a broad set of open-weight and commercial generators (\S\ref{sec:data}--\ref{sec:method}). (3)~We show the
pre-LLM baseline is structured, varying more than threefold across scientific fields, and that arXiv
abstracts moved into the LLM region after 2022 far beyond their pre-trend, with a field-level dose pattern,
placebo breaks and a parliamentary-speech control (\S\ref{sec:results}). (4)~We show that marker-word
counts, the standard lexical measure, peaked and receded while \mso{} did not, and that the population most
often falsely flagged by perplexity-based detectors, English-language learners, is the \emph{least}
machine-like under style-based overlap.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Prevalence of LLM-assisted writing.}
Excess-vocabulary methods compare word frequencies with a counterfactual extrapolated from pre-LLM years
\citep{kobak2025, holzwarth2026, lee2026}; distributional methods fit a mixture of human and LLM word
distributions \citep{liang2024mapping, liang2024monitoring, liang2025widespread, geng2024}; classifier- and
detector-based studies label documents \citep{lin2025, brooks2024, sun2025}. All target the share of text
produced with LLM help. The closest designs to ours are \citet{bentley2026}, who build an LLM-associated
style index for arXiv abstracts from a small set of markers and test for a post-2023 break, and
\citet{chang2026}, who analyse a decade of convergence between human writing and machine patterns. We differ
in the estimand, a distributional overlap in a multi-feature style space whose pre-LLM level is itself the
object of study, and in excluding content words entirely: authors react to publicised markers
\citep{geng2025}, so lexical markers are a moving target.

\paragraph{Homogenization and human adoption.}
Writing with instruction-tuned models reduces content diversity \citep{padmakumar2024, doshi2024,
anderson2024homog}; LLM rewriting compresses stylistic variance \citep{sourati2025}; preference tuning reduces
output diversity \citep{kirk2024, wu2025monoculture} and plausibly produces LLM-overused words
\citep{juzek2025}. Humans adopt LLM vocabulary in speech \citep{yakura2024, anderson2025spoken} and academic
writing \citep{geng2025, lin2025}. If LLM style partly reflects preferences that human raters already held,
some pre-LLM human writing should already sit inside the LLM region, which is what \mso{} measures.

\paragraph{Stylistic differences between humans and LLMs.}
Instruction-tuned LLMs differ systematically from humans on lexico-grammatical features \citep{reinhart2025,
munozortiz2024}, but genre explains more stylistic variance than source \citep{rallapalli2026}, so any overlap
measure must be genre-specific. Distribution-level comparisons such as MAUVE \citep{pillutla2021} and coverage
frameworks \citep{yang2026coverage} evaluate models against human text; we reverse the direction and track
where human documents fall relative to an LLM region over time.

\paragraph{Detection and perception.}
Untrained readers judge GPT-3 text at chance \citep{clark2021}; people rely on flawed cues
\citep{jakesch2023}; linguists and scientific reviewers misclassify abstracts \citep{casal2023, gao2023}.
Detectors are evadable \citep{sadasivan2023, krishna2023, dugan2024raid} and their false positives fall
disproportionately on non-native writing \citep{liang2023detectors, park2026}. We rely on these published
findings for claims about human perception and collect no human judgements.

\section{Machine-Style Overlap}
\label{sec:mso}

Fix a genre $G$ and a feature map $\phi$ from documents to style vectors. Let $H_0$ be the distribution of
$\phi(d)$ for human documents of genre $G$ written before LLM writing assistance was widely available (here,
before 30 November 2022), and $L$ the distribution of $\phi(d)$ for LLM output in the same genre, a mixture
over generators and prompting styles with equal weights. For any population $S$ of documents,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSO}_G(S) = \Pr_{d\sim S}\big[\, p_L(\phi(d)) \ge p_{H_0}(\phi(d)) \,\big].
\end{equation}
A document is \emph{in the LLM region} if its style is at least as likely under LLM output as under pre-LLM
human writing. With equal class priors the Bayes posterior is $\eta(x) = p_L(x)/(p_L(x)+p_{H_0}(x))$, so
$\mathrm{MSO}_G(S) = \Pr_S[\eta(\phi(d)) \ge 1/2]$.

\paragraph{Pre-LLM overlap is a population property.}
For $S = H_0$, \mso{} is the false-positive rate of the Bayes-optimal classifier, and the Bayes error is
$\tfrac12\big(\mathrm{MSO}_G(H_0) + \Pr_L[\eta < \tfrac12]\big)$. The baseline is therefore the human half of an
irreducible error under $\phi$: zero only if human and LLM styles are perfectly separable, and rising towards
$\tfrac12$ as the distributions coincide. Prevalence studies that subtract a pre-period share as
``misclassification'' remove exactly this quantity.

\paragraph{Overlap after 2022.}
For later populations $S_t$, $\mathrm{MSO}_G(S_t)$ measures how much of the genre at time $t$ falls in a region
defined relative to \emph{pre-LLM} human writing. It is agnostic to authorship by design: an increase is
consistent with undisclosed LLM use, with humans adopting LLM style, or both, and we do not separate them.

\paragraph{Estimation.}
We estimate $\eta$ with calibrated classifiers trained on balanced samples of pre-LLM human and LLM documents.
Finite-sample classifiers are not Bayes-optimal, so we report two model families, leave-one-generator-out
regions and repeated training draws, and treat their spread as part of the uncertainty.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Human text.}
\emph{arXiv abstracts}: a stratified random sample drawn through the arXiv API: for each of 8 fields (by
primary category) and each month from January 2015 to September 2026, all papers in one randomly placed
window of consecutive submission days, with window length scaled to field size
(\R{arxiv_instrument.n_train_per_class} pre-ChatGPT abstracts per class enter training; 159,102 abstracts
in total).
\emph{Parliamentary speech}: the Australian Hansard database \citep{katz2025hansard}, restricted to spoken
speeches (excluding interjections and written questions), up to 150 randomly sampled per month from January
2015 to July 2025. Spoken parliamentary speech is our less-exposed control genre.
\emph{Student essays}: PERSUADE~2.0 \citep{crossley2024persuade}, argumentative essays by US students in grades
6--12 on 15 prompts, written before 2020, with English-language-learner (ELL) status for each writer.
In every genre half of the pre-cutoff documents (the \emph{train pool}) train the classifier and seed LLM
prompts; \mso{} is measured only on the remaining documents.

\paragraph{LLM text.}
Seven open-weight models run locally through Ollama (Llama~3.1~8B, Qwen3~8B, Gemma~3~12B, Mistral~7B,
gpt-oss~20B, Phi-4~14B, Granite~3.3~8B; temperature 0.8, fixed seeds) each received three prompt styles per
genre: \emph{P1} write the text from a title or topic; \emph{P2} improve the wording of a human draft
(LLM-assisted revision); \emph{P3} write the text and ``avoid typical AI phrasing'', the instruction a writer
evading detection would give. Prompts were seeded only from train-pool documents, 150 per model and prompt.
For abstracts we add the unattacked generations in RAID \citep{dugan2024raid}, including GPT-4 and ChatGPT,
and abstracts from a 2026 frontier model (Claude) answering the same prompt instances. Each generator
contributes at most 450 texts, so no single source dominates the region.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Cleaning.}
All texts, human and LLM alike, are normalised identically: chat wrappers (``Here's the essay:''), markdown,
headings and trailing notes are removed; curly quotes, non-breaking hyphens and non-breaking spaces are mapped
to ASCII; every text is truncated to its first 250 words. Without this step formatting artefacts alone
separate LLM output from human text.

\paragraph{Features.}
Features are deliberately topic-robust: per-token rates of closed-class words (adpositions, auxiliaries,
conjunctions, determiners, particles, pronouns), coarse part-of-speech trigrams, dependency labels and
punctuation marks, plus the mean and standard deviation of sentence length, mean word length and MTLD lexical
diversity \citep{mccarthy2010}, computed with spaCy 3.8 (\texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm}). No content words are
used. Features present in fewer than 1\% of training documents are dropped.

\paragraph{Classifiers.}
The primary instrument is L2-regularised logistic regression ($C{=}0.1$) on standardised features with
isotonic calibration (5-fold); the second is gradient-boosted trees, calibrated the same way. Classes are
balanced by subsampling, so a calibrated probability of $0.5$ corresponds to a likelihood ratio of one.
Cross-validation is grouped so that a revised text and its human source, and all speeches by one
parliamentarian, stay in the same fold.

\paragraph{Time series.}
We compute monthly \mso{} and fit a segmented regression with a level shift and a slope change at December
2022, weighted by monthly sample size, with Newey--West errors (6 lags). The pre-registered quantity is the
\emph{excess}: the gap between the fitted post-2022 series and the extrapolated pre-2022 trend at June 2025,
the last month covered by both treatment and control. Placebo breaks are placed at November 2019 and
November 2020 within the pre-LLM data. For comparison with lexical methods we run the same model on the share
of abstracts containing at least one published LLM marker word (\emph{delve}, \emph{underscore},
\emph{showcase}, \emph{meticulous}, \emph{intricate}, \emph{intricacies}, \emph{commendable}, \emph{boast};
taken from \citealp{kobak2025, gray2024, yakura2024}).

\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Human and LLM styles are separable but overlap}
In all three genres the instrument separates pre-LLM human text from LLM text well (cross-validated AUC
\R{arxiv_instrument.cv_auc_all} for abstracts, \R{hansard_instrument.cv_auc_all} for speech,
\R{essay_instrument.cv_auc_all} for essays), and the region generalises to generators it never saw (recall on
held-out generators in Appendix~\ref{app:lomo}). The hardest case everywhere is LLM revision of a human draft
(P2), the setting closest to everyday AI-assisted writing. Separability is high but not complete, and the
remainder is the overlap we study.

\subsection{Pre-LLM overlap is structured, not noise (H1)}
Before ChatGPT, \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% (95\% CI \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.lo}--%
\R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.hi}) of arXiv abstracts already fell in the LLM region, as did
\R{hansard_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% of parliamentary speeches and \R{essay_mso_by_group.all.mso}\% of student
essays. The arXiv baseline differs sharply by field (Figure~\ref{fig:fields}, left; $\chi^2$ test
$p\R{arxiv_its.baseline_field_heterogeneity.p}$), from \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.astro-ph.mso}\% in astrophysics
to \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.cs.mso}\% in computer science and \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.econ.mso}\% in
economics. Estimator noise would be uniform across fields; this structure is what a property of the writing
predicts. It was also rising slowly before ChatGPT (\R{arxiv_its.all.pre_slope_per_year} points per year).

\subsection{Abstracts moved into the region after 2022 (H2--H4)}
Yearly arXiv \mso{} was \R{arxiv_yearly.2018.mso}\% in 2018 and \R{arxiv_yearly.2022.mso}\% in 2022, then
\R{arxiv_yearly.2023.mso}\%, \R{arxiv_yearly.2024.mso}\% and \R{arxiv_yearly.2025.mso}\% in 2023--2025
(Figure~\ref{fig:ts}a). In June 2025 the series sat \R{arxiv_its.all.excess_at_2025-06} points above its
extrapolated pre-2022 trend (95\% CI \R{arxiv_its.all.excess_lo}--\R{arxiv_its.all.excess_hi}). Placebo breaks
in the pre-LLM period show no comparable shift (level shifts \R{arxiv_its.all.placebo.2019-11.level_shift} and
\R{arxiv_its.all.placebo.2020-11.level_shift} points, $p=\R{arxiv_its.all.placebo.2019-11.level_shift_p}$ and
$\R{arxiv_its.all.placebo.2020-11.level_shift_p}$).
\emph{Control.} Parliamentary speech moved far less (\R{hansard_its.all.excess_at_2025-06} points, CI
\R{hansard_its.all.excess_lo}--\R{hansard_its.all.excess_hi}); the difference between genres is
\R{control_difference.difference} points (CI \R{control_difference.lo}--\R{control_difference.hi},
$p\R{control_difference.p}$).
\emph{Dose.} The excess is largest in computer science (\R{arxiv_its.cs.excess_at_2025-06} points) and
smallest in mathematics (\R{arxiv_its.math.excess_at_2025-06}) and astrophysics
(\R{arxiv_its.astro-ph.excess_at_2025-06}) (Figure~\ref{fig:fields}, right), the same ordering reported for
LLM use by prevalence studies \citep{liang2024mapping}. The rise continued into 2026
(\R{arxiv_yearly.2026.mso}\% through September).

\paragraph{The region must include current models.}
A 2026 frontier model writes largely outside the region defined by older generators: with it held out, the
region captures only \R{arxiv_lomo.claude-opus-5.5.recall_on_unseen_model}\% of its abstracts, against
\R{arxiv_lomo.raid-gpt4.recall_on_unseen_model}\% for held-out GPT-4. In a preliminary run without the
frontier model, arXiv \mso{} appeared to fall in 2026; with it, the rise continues. Newer models no longer
sound like ``classic'' LLM text, so an overlap measure defined only by older models will understate recent
movement. We therefore include the newest generator available to us and report leave-one-out results for all.

\subsection{Marker words recede; style overlap does not}
The share of abstracts with a marker word also jumped after 2022 (Figure~\ref{fig:ts}c), but it peaked in 2024
and fell back towards its pre-2022 trend (excess \R{arxiv_marker_its.excess_at_2025-06} points in June 2025),
consistent with authors avoiding publicised markers \citep{geng2025}. \mso{} remained far above trend. The two
measures are weakly related at the document level (point-biserial $r=\R{arxiv_marker_vs_mso.corr_marker_inregion}$;
only \R{arxiv_marker_vs_mso.share_inregion_with_marker}\% of in-region abstracts contain a marker word):
lexical counts capture the visible tip of a broader stylistic shift.

\subsection{Chat tuning makes the style}
A region defined only by chat-tuned generators captures \R{arxiv_base_vs_chat.raid-gpt2.share_in_region}\% of
GPT-2 abstracts and \R{arxiv_base_vs_chat.raid-mistral.share_in_region}\% of base Mistral abstracts, but
\R{arxiv_base_vs_chat.raid-mistral-chat.share_in_region}\% of the same model after chat tuning. What humans hear
as ``sounding like AI'' is largely a product of instruction and preference tuning, in line with
\citet{kirk2024} and \citet{juzek2025}.

\subsection{Learners are the least machine-like writers (H5)}
Essays by English-language learners are less likely to fall in the LLM region than essays by their peers
(\R{essay_mso_by_group.ELL.mso}\% vs \R{essay_mso_by_group.non-ELL.mso}\%; Figure~\ref{fig:learners}). Because
few learner essays cross the threshold, the thresholded contrast is fragile: across ten training draws the
learner share is always lower (\R{essay_seed_stability.share_seeds_ell_lower}\% of draws), but the
prompt- and grade-adjusted odds ratio (median \R{essay_seed_stability.odds_ratio_median}, range
\R{essay_seed_stability.odds_ratio_min}--\R{essay_seed_stability.odds_ratio_max}) reaches $p<0.05$ in only
\R{essay_seed_stability.share_seeds_p_below_05}\% of draws. The continuous classifier margin, which uses every
essay, gives a clear answer: relative to peers' essays on the same prompt and grade, learner essays differ by
\R{essay_ell_continuous.ell_coef_sd} standard deviations on the margin, away from the LLM region (95\% CI
\R{essay_ell_continuous.coef_lo} to \R{essay_ell_continuous.coef_hi}). A different instrument built on sentence
embeddings agrees (\R{essay_embedding_instrument.ELL.mso}\% vs \R{essay_embedding_instrument.non-ELL.mso}\%).
Perplexity-based detectors flag non-native writing because it is predictable \citep{liang2023detectors}; the
style region is defined by richer vocabulary, longer words and more uniform sentences, which learners use
less. The same text can be ``AI-like'' to one instrument and ``human-like'' to another.

\subsection{Instrument agreement and genre specificity}
The two classifier families agree on population-level \mso{} (\R{arxiv_instrument.mso_logreg}\% vs
\R{arxiv_instrument.mso_gbm}\% for abstracts) more than on individual documents (Cohen's
$\kappa=\R{arxiv_instrument.kappa_logreg_vs_gbm}$ for abstracts, \R{essay_instrument.kappa_logreg_vs_gbm} for
essays). \mso{} is also genre-specific: the essay instrument applied to the out-of-genre human essays released
by \citet{liang2023detectors} places \R{essay_liang.Real TOEFL.mso}\% of TOEFL and
\R{essay_liang.Real College Essays.mso}\% of US college essays in the region, near chance. Population-level
overlap is stable; any single document's label is not, which bears directly on judging individual writers.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What ``sounds like AI'' means.}
Three results jointly reframe the perception that everything now sounds like AI. First, a measurable share of
human writing sat in the LLM region before LLMs existed, and that share depends on genre and field: formal,
noun-heavy registers were closest to what instruction-tuned models produce. Second, scientific abstracts
moved substantially further into the region after 2022 while parliamentary speech did not, so the perception
reflects a real change in some writing, not only heightened suspicion. Third, the style region is created by
chat tuning rather than by language modelling as such, which is consistent with LLM style amplifying
preferences that human raters already held \citep{juzek2025}.

\paragraph{Why marker words mislead.}
Marker words are what readers notice and what authors can edit out. Their rise and fall alongside a persistent
rise in \mso{} suggests that the visible markers were removed while the underlying register remained. Studies
that track markers may therefore understate continued LLM influence in 2025--2026. The frontier-model result
points the same way from the other side: current models no longer produce the markers, or the classic register,
that readers learned to associate with AI, so ``sounds like AI'' is itself a moving target.

\paragraph{A self-test.}
This paper was drafted by an AI system (see AI Use). Scored by the abstract instrument,
\R{selftest.share_paragraphs_in_region}\% of its own prose paragraphs fall in the LLM region, compared with
\R{selftest.mso_cs_2026_humans}\% of human-attributed computer-science abstracts from 2026. Paragraphs of a paper
are not abstracts, so this comparison is illustrative only, but it makes the paper's point concrete: by this
measure, most 2026 computer-science abstracts read as more machine-like than an AI-drafted paper about them.

\paragraph{A feedback loop, as a hypothesis.}
If human writing moves into the LLM region and later models are trained on it, ``human'' training data stops
being an independent anchor. Model collapse under recursive training is documented in controlled settings
\citep{shumailov2024}, but accumulating real data limits it \citep{gerstgrasser2024}. Our results show the
human side of such a loop moving in one genre; they do not show the loop.

\paragraph{Implications for detection.}
Population-level \mso{} is stable across instruments while document-level labels are not, and the writers most
exposed to perplexity-detector false positives are the least machine-like on style. Both findings argue
against using any single score to judge an individual writer, in education or elsewhere.

\section*{Limitations}
\mso{} depends on the feature map: a document can be inside the region under style features and outside it
under perplexity, as the learner result shows. Post-2022 ``human'' documents may be partly LLM-written, so
\mso{} cannot separate LLM use from human adoption of LLM style, and no causal claim is made. Most local
generators are mid-sized open-weight models; commercial models enter through RAID abstracts and one frontier
model, whose decoding settings we could not control. The arXiv sample is a stratified random sample, not the
full archive. The learner analysis is cross-sectional and rests on few in-region learner essays at the
threshold. Parliamentary speech is less exposed to LLM writing but not unexposed.
