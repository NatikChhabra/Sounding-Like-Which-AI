\title{After the Delve Fades: Scientific Abstracts Keep Moving Toward LLM Style}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Corpus studies of LLM-assisted writing estimate how much text was produced with large language model (LLM)
help. We ask a complementary question: how much writing, whoever wrote it, falls on the LLM side of a
stylistic boundary, and how much already did before ChatGPT. We define \textbf{Machine-Style Overlap}
(\mso{}), the share of a genre's documents that a calibrated classifier on content-word-free style features
places at least as close to LLM output as to pre-ChatGPT human writing. In \R{corpus_stats.arxiv_n_docs} arXiv
abstracts (2015--2026), \mso{} was \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% before ChatGPT, varying from
\R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.astro-ph.mso}\% in astrophysics to \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.econ.mso}\% in
economics, and reached \R{arxiv_yearly.2026.mso}\% in 2026. By June 2025 it was \R{arxiv_its.all.excess}
percentage points above its pre-ChatGPT trend, a gap that survives length, notation, topic, threshold and
punctuation controls and is absent in placebo and drift tests; Australian parliamentary speech did not move
measurably. Marker words such as \emph{delve} peaked in 2024 and returned almost to trend, while \mso{} kept
rising. The measured trajectory depends on which generators define the LLM side: a region built only from 2023
models shows the shift receding in 2026, whereas regions that include current models show it continuing.
Marker-word counts therefore understate how far scientific abstracts have moved toward LLM style. A secondary
analysis finds that a perplexity-based detector reproduces a published bias against TOEFL essays yet flags US
school learners less than their peers: detector bias depends on the population, not on non-native authorship.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

LLM influence on writing is easy to detect in aggregate but hard to detect in individual documents. Corpus
studies find clear traces of LLM-assisted writing in scientific abstracts, peer reviews, press releases and
encyclopedia articles \citep{kobak2025, liang2024mapping, liang2024monitoring, liang2025widespread,
brooks2024}, and LLM-associated words have become more frequent in unscripted speech \citep{yakura2024, anderson2025spoken}. Yet
readers are poor at telling human from machine text \citep{clark2021, jakesch2023, casal2023}, detectors can be
evaded \citep{sadasivan2023, krishna2023, dugan2024raid}, and some detectors have flagged non-native writers'
human text as AI-generated at high rates \citep{liang2023detectors}. No existing measure says how much
writing falls on the machine side of that boundary, in which genres, or since when.

Existing measurements answer a \emph{prevalence} question: what fraction of a corpus was produced or modified
with an LLM? We answer an \emph{overlap} question: what fraction of a genre's writing is stylistically
closer to LLM output than to pre-ChatGPT human writing? The two questions read one number differently: the
small ``LLM share'' that prevalence estimators report for text written before ChatGPT, which studies treat as
consistent with false positives \citep{liang2024mapping}, subtract as baseline misclassification
\citep{lin2025}, or use to calibrate detector thresholds \citep{brooks2024}. For a fixed feature map and
a fixed set of reference generators, that share is a property of the two text distributions, and its change
over time is our object of study.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1)~We define Machine-Style Overlap (\mso{}), a classify-and-count quantity \citep{forman2008} whose
pre-ChatGPT value estimates the human-side component of a Bayes error (\S\ref{sec:mso}).
(2)~We estimate it in three genres with content-word-free features and \R{arxiv_instrument.n_generators}
reference generators, from 2023 commercial systems to a 2026 frontier model (\S\ref{sec:data}--\ref{sec:method}).
(3)~We show that arXiv abstracts moved into the LLM region far beyond their pre-ChatGPT trend, more in
fields with higher reported LLM adoption, with no shift at placebo dates, no drift in an out-of-sample
placebo, and no measurable change in parliamentary speech (\S\ref{sec:shift}).
(4)~We show that marker-word counts peaked and receded while \mso{} did not (\S\ref{sec:markers}), and that
the trajectory depends on how current the reference generators are (\S\ref{sec:gensets}).
(5)~We report two secondary results: stylistic dispersion of abstracts declined steadily since 2015 without a
post-ChatGPT acceleration (\S\ref{sec:dispersion}), and, contrary to our pre-registered hypothesis, essays by
English-language learners are \emph{less} machine-like than their peers' essays, mostly because of
proficiency (\S\ref{sec:learners}).
Our analysis plan, with five hypotheses (H1: pre-ChatGPT \mso{} is non-zero and differs by field; H2: arXiv
\mso{} rises above its pre-ChatGPT trend; H3: more than in a less-exposed genre; H4: more in computer science
than in mathematics; H5: learner essays have higher \mso{}), was committed to version control before any
result was computed; deviations are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Prevalence of LLM-assisted writing.}
Excess-vocabulary methods compare word frequencies with a counterfactual extrapolated from pre-LLM years
\citep{kobak2025, holzwarth2026, lee2026}; distributional methods fit a mixture of human and LLM word
distributions \citep{liang2024mapping, liang2024monitoring, liang2025widespread, geng2024}; classifier- and
detector-based studies label documents \citep{lin2025, brooks2024, sun2025, khan2025}. All target the share
of text produced with LLM help. Closest to ours, \citet{bentley2026} track an LLM-associated style index for
arXiv abstracts built from a few markers, and \citet{chang2026} analyse a decade of convergence between human
writing and machine patterns. We differ in the estimand, a distributional overlap whose pre-ChatGPT level is
itself of interest, and in excluding content words from the features: authors react to publicised markers
\citep{geng2025}, so lexical markers are a moving target.

\paragraph{Homogenization and human adoption.}
Writing or ideating with instruction-tuned models reduces content diversity across users
\citep{padmakumar2024, doshi2024, anderson2024homog}; LLM rewriting compresses stylistic variance \citep{sourati2025}; preference tuning reduces
output diversity \citep{kirk2024, wu2025monoculture} and plausibly produces LLM-overused words
\citep{juzek2025}. LLM-associated vocabulary has grown in human speech \citep{yakura2024, anderson2025spoken}, academic authors
adapt their LLM use once overused words are publicised \citep{geng2025}, and adoption of LLM revision varies
across disciplines and author groups \citep{lin2025}. \mso{} quantifies how much human writing shares LLM style at a given time;
a non-zero pre-ChatGPT value is necessary for, but not evidence of, a preference-amplification account.

\paragraph{Style of human and LLM text.}
LLM text differs systematically from human text in lexico-grammatical features \citep{reinhart2025,
munozortiz2024, berbersardinha2024, herbold2023} and in recurring part-of-speech templates \citep{shaib2024},
but genre shapes stylistic features more strongly than source does \citep{rallapalli2026}, so any overlap measure must be
genre-specific. Distribution-level comparisons such as MAUVE \citep{pillutla2021} and coverage frameworks
\citep{yang2026coverage} evaluate models against human text; we reverse the direction and track where human
documents fall relative to an LLM region over time.

\paragraph{Detection and perception.}
Untrained readers judge GPT-3 text at chance \citep{clark2021}; people rely on flawed cues
\citep{jakesch2023}; linguists and scientific reviewers misclassify abstracts \citep{casal2023, gao2023}.
Zero-shot detectors use token-likelihood statistics such as perplexity ratios and probability curvature
\citep{hans2024binoculars, bao2024fastdetectgpt}, can be evaded \citep{sadasivan2023, krishna2023,
dugan2024raid}, need more text to succeed as machine and human distributions converge
\citep{chakraborty2024}, and have produced false positives concentrated on non-native writing
\citep{liang2023detectors}; on non-native academic manuscripts their false-positive rates range widely and
shift with professional editing \citep{park2026}. We rely on published studies for claims about human
perception and collect no human judgements.

\section{Machine-Style Overlap}
\label{sec:mso}

Fix a genre and a feature map $\phi$ from documents to style vectors. Let $P_{\text{pre}}$ be the distribution
of $\phi(d)$ for human documents written before ChatGPT's release (30 November 2022; ``pre-ChatGPT''
throughout), and $P_{\text{LLM}}$ the distribution for LLM output in the same genre, an equal-weight mixture
over a reference set of generators and prompting styles chosen by us. For any population $S$ of documents,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSO}(S) = \Pr_{d\sim S}\big[\, p_{\text{LLM}}(\phi(d)) \ge p_{\text{pre}}(\phi(d)) \,\big].
\end{equation}
With equal priors the Bayes posterior is $\eta(x) = p_{\text{LLM}}(x)/(p_{\text{LLM}}(x)+p_{\text{pre}}(x))$, so
$\mathrm{MSO}(S) = \Pr_S[\eta(\phi(d)) \ge \tfrac12]$: \mso{} is the flag rate of the Bayes-optimal
classifier, i.e.\ classify-and-count \citep{forman2008} with a calibrated threshold.

\paragraph{Pre-ChatGPT overlap.}
For $S = P_{\text{pre}}$, \mso{} is the Bayes classifier's false-positive rate, and the Bayes error is
$\tfrac12\big(\mathrm{MSO}(P_{\text{pre}}) + \Pr_{\text{LLM}}[\eta < \tfrac12]\big)$, which is zero only when
the two styles are separable and reaches $\tfrac12$ when they coincide. The pre-ChatGPT baseline is the human
component of that error. Prevalence studies that subtract a pre-period share as misclassification remove the
analogue of this quantity under their own feature map.

\paragraph{Overlap after ChatGPT.}
For later populations $S_t$, $\mathrm{MSO}(S_t)$ measures how much writing at time $t$ falls in a region defined
relative to pre-ChatGPT human writing. It is agnostic to authorship: a rise is consistent with undisclosed LLM
use, with humans adopting LLM style, or both, and we do not separate them.

\paragraph{Estimation.}
A finite classifier only approximates $\eta$, and its flag rate is neither an upper nor a lower bound on the
Bayes quantity. We therefore treat \mso{} as instrument-dependent and report how it varies across two
classifier families, held-out generators, generator sets and thresholds; confidence intervals in the text are
document-level bootstrap or regression intervals and do not include this instrument variation.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Human text.}
\emph{arXiv abstracts.} For each of \R{corpus_stats.arxiv_n_fields} archives (cs, math, stat, eess, q-bio,
econ, astro-ph, cond-mat; by primary category) and each month from January 2015 to September 2026, we fetched
all papers submitted in one randomly placed window of consecutive days (length scaled to archive size) through
the arXiv API and drew up to 200 at random, so every archive-month has the same design weight. Because the API
returns the latest abstract version, papers first submitted before ChatGPT but revised after it are excluded.
After removing abstracts under 50 words, \R{corpus_stats.arxiv_n_docs} abstracts remain.
\emph{Parliamentary speech.} The Australian House of Representatives Hansard database
\citep{katz2023scidata, katz2025hansard}, restricted to spoken
speeches (no interjections or written questions), up to 150 per month from January 2015 to July 2025
(\R{corpus_stats.hansard_n_docs} speeches). Hansard is a lightly edited transcript; we treat it as less exposed
to LLM writing, not unexposed.
\emph{Student essays.} PERSUADE~2.0 \citep{crossley2024persuade}: \R{corpus_stats.essay_n_docs} argumentative
essays by US students in grades 6--12 on \R{essay_ell_counts.n_prompts} prompts, written before ChatGPT, with
English-language-learner (ELL) status and a holistic quality score for each essay. These are school learners,
not the TOEFL population of \citet{liang2023detectors}.
In every genre half of the pre-ChatGPT documents (the \emph{train pool}) train the classifier and seed LLM
prompts; \mso{} is measured only on the remaining documents, and any document used as a prompt seed is kept
out of measurement.

\paragraph{LLM text.}
Eight generators answered three prompt styles per genre, 150 times each: seven open-weight models run locally
(Llama~3.1~8B, Qwen3~8B, Gemma~3~12B, Mistral~7B, gpt-oss~20B, Phi-4~14B, Granite~3.3~8B; temperature 0.8,
fixed seeds) and Claude Opus~5.5, a 2026 frontier model, through sub-agent sessions without control over
decoding. Claude is also the system that drafted this paper (see AI Use). The prompt styles are \emph{P1}
write the text from a title or topic; \emph{P2} improve the wording of a human draft (LLM-assisted revision);
\emph{P3} write the text and ``avoid typical AI phrasing'', a plausible evasion instruction. Prompts were seeded
only from train-pool documents. For abstracts we add the RAID generations without adversarial attacks
\citep{dugan2024raid} from six chat models (GPT-4, ChatGPT, and chat versions of Cohere, Llama, Mistral and
MPT) and, for one analysis only, five base models. Each generator contributes at most 450 texts to training,
so no source dominates.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Cleaning.}
All texts, human and LLM, pass through the same normalisation: chat wrappers (``Here's the essay:''),
markdown, headings and trailing notes are removed; curly quotes, non-breaking hyphens and spaces, and
mis-decoded Windows-1252 characters are mapped to plain equivalents; LaTeX math becomes a placeholder token,
LaTeX dashes (\texttt{--}, \texttt{---}) become typographic en and em dashes, and formatting commands keep
only their content. The LaTeX step matters: human arXiv abstracts write dashes as \texttt{--} while chat
models emit Unicode dashes, which would otherwise separate the two sources by encoding alone. Every text is
truncated to its first 250 words.

\paragraph{Features.}
Features exclude content words to limit topic sensitivity: per-token rates of closed-class words
(adpositions, auxiliaries, conjunctions, determiners, particles, pronouns), coarse part-of-speech trigrams,
dependency labels and punctuation marks, plus mean and standard deviation of sentence length, mean word length
and MTLD lexical diversity \citep{mccarthy2010}, computed with spaCy~3.8 (\texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm}).
Features present in fewer than 1\% of training documents are dropped.

\paragraph{Classifiers.}
The primary classifier is L2-regularised logistic regression ($C{=}0.1$) on standardised features with
isotonic calibration; the second is gradient-boosted trees, calibrated the same way. Classes are balanced, so a
calibrated probability of 0.5 corresponds to a likelihood ratio of one. Cross-validation and calibration folds
are grouped so that a revised text and its human source, and all speeches by one parliamentarian, never fall
in different folds. We call a trained, calibrated classifier with its 0.5 threshold an \emph{instrument}.

\paragraph{Time series.}
We compute monthly \mso{} and fit an interrupted time-series model: weighted least squares with a level shift
and a slope change at December 2022 and autocorrelation-robust (Newey--West, 6 lags) standard errors. Our
primary quantity, the \emph{trend gap}, is the fitted post-ChatGPT value minus the extrapolated pre-ChatGPT
trend in June 2025, a month fixed in code before any time-series result and within the period covered by both
corpora (Hansard has no sittings from April to June 2025, which the model interpolates). Placebo breaks are
placed at November 2019 and November 2020. A drift placebo trains the instrument on 2015--2018 human
abstracts only and tests for a gap in mid-2022 over the 2015--2018 trend, before any LLM writing assistance:
an instrument that merely ages would produce one. For comparison with lexical methods we model the share of
abstracts containing a published marker word (\emph{delve}, \emph{underscore}, \emph{showcase},
\emph{meticulous}, \emph{intricate}, \emph{intricacies}, \emph{commendable}, \emph{boast};
\citealp{kobak2025, gray2024, yakura2024}).

\subsection{Separable, but not completely}
The instruments separate pre-ChatGPT human text from LLM text well (cross-validated AUC, where 1 is perfect
separation: \R{arxiv_instrument.cv_auc_all} for abstracts, \R{hansard_instrument.cv_auc_all} for speech,
\R{essay_instrument.cv_auc_all} for essays). Held out from training, older generators are still recognised
(arXiv recall \R{arxiv_lomo.raid-cohere-chat.recall_on_unseen_model}--\R{arxiv_lomo.raid-gpt4.recall_on_unseen_model}\%;
Appendix~\ref{app:lomo}), but the 2026 model is not: only \R{arxiv_lomo.claude-opus-5.5.recall_on_unseen_model}\%
of its abstracts and \R{essay_lomo.claude-opus-5.5.recall_on_unseen_model}\% of its essays fall in a region
built without it. At least one current model writes outside the region defined by its predecessors.

\subsection{Pre-ChatGPT overlap is structured (H1)}
Before ChatGPT, \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% (95\% CI \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.lo}--%
\R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.hi}) of arXiv abstracts fell in the LLM region, as did
\R{hansard_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% of speeches and \R{essay_mso_by_group.all.mso}\% of student essays. The
arXiv baseline varies by archive (Figure~\ref{fig:fields}; $\chi^2$ $p\R{arxiv_its.baseline_field_heterogeneity.p}$),
from \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.astro-ph.mso}\% in astrophysics to \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.cs.mso}\% in
computer science and \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.econ.mso}\% in economics. The ordering is similar under the
gradient-boosted instrument and among abstracts without math (astrophysics
\R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.nomath astro-ph.mso}\%, economics \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.nomath econ.mso}\%), so
it is not an artefact of notation. Field differences far exceed sampling error; they may reflect the writing,
classifier fit that varies by field, or both. Overall \mso{} was also rising slowly before ChatGPT
(\R{arxiv_its.all.pre_slope_per_year}\pp{} per year).

\subsection{Abstracts moved into the region (H2--H4)}
\label{sec:shift}
Yearly arXiv \mso{} ranged from \R{arxiv_yearly.2016.mso}\% to \R{arxiv_yearly.2019.mso}\% in 2015--2021 and
was \R{arxiv_yearly.2022.mso}\% in 2022, then \R{arxiv_yearly.2023.mso}\%, \R{arxiv_yearly.2024.mso}\%,
\R{arxiv_yearly.2025.mso}\% and \R{arxiv_yearly.2026.mso}\% in 2023--2026 (Figure~\ref{fig:ts}a). The trend gap
in June 2025 is \R{arxiv_its.all.excess}\pp{} (95\% CI \R{arxiv_its.all.excess_lo}--\R{arxiv_its.all.excess_hi}).
Table~\ref{tab:robust} shows it under every check we ran: other thresholds, abstracts without math, abstracts
that never mention language models, a length-reweighted series (median abstract length grew from
\R{arxiv_length_by_year.2015.median_words} to \R{arxiv_length_by_year.2026.median_words} words), and an
instrument without any dash feature. Placebo breaks show no comparable gap, and the drift placebo, which would
reveal an instrument that simply ages, finds none ($\R{arxiv_drift_placebo.excess}$\pp{}, CI
$\R{arxiv_drift_placebo.excess_lo}$ to \R{arxiv_drift_placebo.excess_hi}).
\emph{Comparison genre (H3).} Parliamentary speech did not move measurably (\R{hansard_its.all.excess}\pp{}, CI
$\R{hansard_its.all.excess_lo}$ to \R{hansard_its.all.excess_hi}). With both regions built from the same
eight generators, the arXiv gap exceeds the Hansard gap by \R{control_difference.difference}\pp{} (CI
\R{control_difference.lo}--\R{control_difference.hi}).
\emph{Field gradient (H4).} The gap is largest in quantitative biology (\R{arxiv_its.q-bio.excess}\pp) and
computer science (\R{arxiv_its.cs.excess}) and smallest in mathematics (\R{arxiv_its.math.excess}) and
astrophysics (\R{arxiv_its.astro-ph.excess}) (Figure~\ref{fig:fields}), the same high-CS, low-mathematics
ordering that prevalence studies report \citep{liang2024mapping}.

\subsection{Marker words recede; style overlap does not}
\label{sec:markers}
The share of abstracts with a marker word rose from \R{arxiv_yearly.2022.marker}\% in 2022 to
\R{arxiv_yearly.2024.marker}\% in 2024 and fell to \R{arxiv_yearly.2026.marker}\% in 2026
(Figure~\ref{fig:ts}c), its June 2025 trend gap only \R{arxiv_its.marker.excess}\pp{}, consistent with authors
avoiding publicised markers \citep{geng2025}. Over the same period \mso{} kept rising. At the document level the
two are related but largely distinct (point-biserial $r=\R{arxiv_marker_vs_mso.corr_marker_inregion}$;
\R{arxiv_marker_vs_mso.share_inregion_with_marker}\% of in-region versus
\R{arxiv_marker_vs_mso.share_outregion_with_marker}\% of out-of-region abstracts contain a marker): marker
words and \mso{} mostly pick out different documents.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the generator set}
\label{sec:gensets}
Because \mso{} is defined relative to reference generators, we rebuilt the region from five generator sets
(Figure~\ref{fig:gensets}, Table~\ref{tab:robust}). All show the rise from 2022 to 2025 and a positive trend
gap. They diverge in 2026: a region built only from RAID's 2023 models gives \R{arxiv_gensets.raid.y2025}\% in
2025 and \R{arxiv_gensets.raid.y2026}\% in 2026, retracing the marker-word curve, while regions that include
2024--2026 models keep rising (\R{arxiv_gensets.local_frontier.y2026}\% in 2026 with the seven local models
plus the 2026 model). Without the 2026 model the series levels off
(\R{arxiv_gensets.no_frontier.y2025}\% to \R{arxiv_gensets.no_frontier.y2026}\%). The pattern is consistent
with abstracts moving away from 2023-era LLM style toward the style of later models, and it implies that any
overlap or detection measure built on older models will understate recent movement. We added the 2026 model
after a preliminary run without it; both versions are reported here.

\subsection{Chat tuning amplifies the style}
A region defined only by chat-tuned generators captures
\R{arxiv_base_models.raid-mistral.share_in_region}--\R{arxiv_base_models.raid-mpt.share_in_region}\% of base-model
abstracts (GPT-2, GPT-3, and base Cohere, Mistral and MPT), against \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% of

\R{arxiv_lomo.raid-cohere-chat.recall_on_unseen_model}\% (Cohere, base
\R{arxiv_base_models.raid-cohere.share_in_region}\%), \R{arxiv_lomo.raid-mistral-chat.recall_on_unseen_model}\%
(Mistral, base \R{arxiv_base_models.raid-mistral.share_in_region}\%) and
\R{arxiv_lomo.raid-mpt-chat.recall_on_unseen_model}\% (MPT, base \R{arxiv_base_models.raid-mpt.share_in_region}\%).
Base models already lie partly inside the region, and chat tuning moves them much further in, in line with
reports that preference tuning reduces output diversity \citep{kirk2024} and may contribute to LLM-overused
words \citep{juzek2025}. RAID's base and chat
generations also differ in prompting and length, so this comparison is indicative.

\subsection{Dispersion: steady, not sudden}
\label{sec:dispersion}
To connect overlap to homogenization we measured, for 1,500 abstracts per year, the mean distance to the year's
centroid in the standardised feature space. Dispersion fell from \R{arxiv_dispersion.2015.dispersion} in 2015 to
\R{arxiv_dispersion.2022.dispersion} in 2022 and \R{arxiv_dispersion.2026.dispersion} in 2026: abstracts have
grown stylistically more uniform, but the decline began well before ChatGPT and did not visibly accelerate
after it. Movement toward LLM style, not loss of variety, is what changed sharply.

\subsection{Learner essays (H5, not supported)}
\label{sec:learners}
We pre-registered that essays by English-language learners would show \emph{higher} \mso{}. They show lower:
\R{essay_mso_by_group.ELL.mso}\% versus \R{essay_mso_by_group.non-ELL.mso}\%
(\R{essay_ell_counts.ell_in_region} of \R{essay_ell_counts.n_ell} learner essays in the region; adjusted odds
ratio \R{essay_ell_adjusted.odds_ratio}, CI \R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_lo}--\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_hi},
lower in all \R{essay_seed_stability.n_seeds} training draws). The difference is mostly proficiency: adjusting
for the holistic essay score reduces the odds ratio to \R{essay_ell_adjusted.odds_ratio_prof} (CI
\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_lo_prof}--\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_hi_prof}) and the continuous classifier margin
from $\R{essay_ell_continuous.ell_coef_sd}$ to $\R{essay_ell_continuous.ell_coef_sd_prof}$ standard
deviations (CI $\R{essay_ell_continuous.coef_lo_prof}$ to $\R{essay_ell_continuous.coef_hi_prof}$). In essays
the LLM region is largely a region of fluent, lexically diverse writing (top LLM-typical features: MTLD,
word length, em dashes). Two other instruments agree in direction (Figure~\ref{fig:learners}): a
sentence-embedding classifier (\R{essay_embedding_instrument.ELL.mso}\% vs
\R{essay_embedding_instrument.non-ELL.mso}\%) and a perplexity-based instrument of the kind used by zero-shot
detectors (\R{essay_perplexity_instrument.ELL.mso}\% vs \R{essay_perplexity_instrument.non-ELL.mso}\%; odds
ratio \R{essay_perplexity_adjusted.odds_ratio}), because these learner essays are \emph{less} predictable
(mean token log-perplexity \R{essay_perplexity_adjusted.mean_logppl_ell} vs
\R{essay_perplexity_adjusted.mean_logppl_nonell} under Qwen2.5-0.5B). The same perplexity instrument does
reproduce the bias reported by \citet{liang2023detectors} on their own data: it flags
\R{essay_liang_perplexity.Real TOEFL.mso}\% of TOEFL essays by non-native test-takers, whose text is highly
predictable (log-perplexity \R{essay_liang_perplexity.Real TOEFL.mean_logppl}), but only
\R{essay_liang_perplexity.Real US 8th grade.mso}\% of US 8th-grade essays
(\R{essay_liang_perplexity.Real US 8th grade.mean_logppl}). One detector thus shows opposite biases in two
learner populations: what it penalises is predictable, formulaic text, which test-takers produce and school
learners, whose essays contain more errors, do not. Detector bias depends on the population, not on
non-native authorship as such.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What ``sounding like AI'' measures.}
Three results bear on the common impression that writing increasingly sounds like AI. A measurable share of
human writing sat in the LLM region before ChatGPT, and that share depends on genre and field. Scientific
abstracts moved substantially further into the region after 2022 while parliamentary speech did not measurably
move, so at least part of the change is in the writing itself. And the style region is strongly amplified by
chat tuning, though base models already lie partly inside it.

\paragraph{Why marker words mislead.}
Marker words are what readers notice and what authors can edit out. Two explanations fit their rise and fall:
authors removing publicised markers, and newer models no longer producing them. Our data cannot separate the
two, but both imply that marker-based tracking may understate how far writing style has moved toward LLM
output in 2025--2026. The generator-set result points the same way: the region of 2023-era LLM style is
emptying while the region of current models is filling.

\paragraph{A feedback loop, as a hypothesis.}
If writing moves into the LLM region and later models are trained on it, ``human'' training data stops being
an independent anchor. Model collapse under recursive training is documented in controlled settings
\citep{shumailov2024}, but is avoided when synthetic data accumulate alongside the original real data
rather than replacing it \citep{gerstgrasser2024}. Our results are
consistent with the human-written side of such a loop moving in one genre; they do not show the loop.

\paragraph{Implications for detection.}
Population-level \mso{} is similar across classifier families (\R{arxiv_instrument.mso_logreg}\% vs
\R{arxiv_instrument.mso_gbm}\% of scored abstracts) and held-out-generator regions, but document-level labels
agree only moderately (Cohen's $\kappa=\R{arxiv_instrument.kappa_logreg_vs_gbm}$), and every instrument we
tried flags fluent student writing more than learner writing. Both findings argue against using any single
score to judge an individual writer.

\section{Conclusion}
Machine-Style Overlap measures how much of a genre's writing sits on the LLM side of a stylistic boundary. In
arXiv abstracts it rose from about \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% before ChatGPT to
\R{arxiv_yearly.2026.mso}\% in 2026, far beyond trend and robust to the controls we could devise, while marker
words came and went and parliamentary speech held still. Measuring that shift requires reference generators as
current as the writing being measured.

\section*{Limitations}
\mso{} is defined relative to a feature map and a researcher-chosen generator set; adding one model changed the
2026 trajectory, and one generator is the system that drafted this paper, run without decoding control.
Punctuation, including dashes, is among the features, although removing dash features leaves the result
unchanged. Post-ChatGPT ``human'' documents may be partly LLM-written, so \mso{} cannot separate LLM use from
human adoption of LLM style, and no causal claim is made. Changes in arXiv's composition (author population,
subfields, submission volume) may contribute despite stratified sampling and the topic and length checks.
The arXiv sample is stratified, not the full archive; all text is English and truncated to 250 words.
Parliamentary speech is less exposed to LLM writing, not unexposed, and differs from abstracts in many ways.
The learner analysis is cross-sectional, rests on \R{essay_ell_counts.ell_in_region} in-region learner essays,
and concerns US school learners. The analysis plan was committed to our own repository, not a third-party
registry.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
The study uses only public datasets under their licences (arXiv metadata CC0; Australian Hansard CC~BY~4.0;
PERSUADE~2.0 CC~BY-NC-SA~4.0; RAID and the data of \citet{liang2023detectors}, MIT) and involves no human
participants. The learner analysis compares groups, never individuals, and we caution against using any
overlap or detector score to judge an individual writer.

\section{Deviations from the analysis plan}
\label{app:deviations}
(1)~arXiv was sampled through the API with a stratified design instead of full harvesting; the first sampler
truncated large windows and was replaced (the paper uses only the second). (2)~Learner data: PERSUADE~2.0
replaced ELLIPSE, which could not be obtained; the human essays of \citet{liang2023detectors} (TOEFL, US
8th-grade and US college essays; the 8th-grade set is distributed only in the authors' GitHub repository and was
added after review) are a different genre from PERSUADE and are used only for the perplexity replication. The
style instrument places \R{essay_liang.Real TOEFL.mso}\% of TOEFL, \R{essay_liang.Real US 8th grade.mso}\% of
8th-grade and \R{essay_liang.Real College Essays.mso}\% of college essays in the PERSUADE-defined region, which
shows how genre-specific the region is. (3)~The self-test of the paper's own text was
dropped. (4)~Added after review: LaTeX and encoding normalisation, grouped calibration, Phi-4, Granite and the
2026 model, generator-set, drift, math, topic, length and dash analyses, the proficiency adjustment, the
perplexity instrument and the dispersion measure. (5)~The plan named cs.CL for one comparison; all of cs is
used. (6)~The June 2025 evaluation month was fixed in code, not in the plan. (7)~Hansard was restricted to
2015--2025 to match the arXiv period. H5 was not supported.
