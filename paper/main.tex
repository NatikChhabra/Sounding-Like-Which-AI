\begin{abstract}
Studies of LLM-assisted writing estimate how much text was produced with LLM help. We ask how much writing, by any author, falls on the LLM side of a stylistic boundary, and which LLMs define that side. We define
\textbf{Machine-Style Overlap} (\mso{}): the share of documents that a calibrated classifier on content-word-free
style features judges at least as likely to be LLM output as pre-ChatGPT human writing. In
\R{corpus_stats.arxiv_n_docs} arXiv abstracts (2015--2026), \mso{} was \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\%
before ChatGPT and by June 2025 stood \R{arxiv_its.all.excess} percentage points above its
pre-ChatGPT trend, well above any placebo or instrument-drift gap. Recent LLMs learn from recent writing, so overlap with them is ambiguous; we compare reference generators by vintage. Against generators whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT,
the gap is smaller but clear (\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.gap} percentage points; \R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.gap_nonparam} measured nonparametrically). With one generator at a time as reference, overlap with all but the newest rises and then falls, peaking later for later generators (exploratory rank correlation
\R{arxiv_vintage_peak.rho}, $n=\R{arxiv_vintage_peak.n}$); for the latest generators the fall is one quarter long. If this pattern persists, a fixed reference set, including a list of marker words such as \emph{delve}, will eventually show the shift receding. In student essays, English
learners' writing is less machine-like than their peers', mostly because of proficiency. A \gptii{} perplexity instrument agrees, although it reproduces a published bias against non-native TOEFL essays.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Corpus studies find traces of LLM-assisted writing in abstracts, peer reviews, press releases and
encyclopedias \citep{kobak2025, liang2024monitoring, liang2024mapping, liang2025widespread,
brooks2024}, and LLM-associated words have become more frequent in unscripted speech \citep{yakura2024,
anderson2025spoken}. Judging individual documents is harder: readers are poor at telling human from machine text
\citep{clark2021, jakesch2023, casal2023}, detectors can be evaded \citep{sadasivan2025, krishna2023,
dugan2024raid}, and some detectors have flagged non-native writers' human text as AI-generated at high rates
\citep{liang2023detectors}.

Existing measurements answer a \emph{prevalence} question: what fraction of a corpus was produced or modified
with an LLM? We ask an \emph{overlap} question: what fraction of a genre's writing, by any author, is stylistically closer to LLM output than to pre-ChatGPT human writing? The two differ in how they handle text written before ChatGPT. Prevalence estimators report a small ``LLM share'' for it, which studies read as consistent with false positives \citep{liang2024mapping}, subtract as baseline misclassification
\citep{lin2025}, or use to calibrate thresholds \citep{brooks2024}. For a fixed feature map and reference set,
we regard that share as a property of the two text distributions and study how it changes over time.

Both questions share a complication that prevalence studies seldom discuss: ``LLM style'' is defined by whichever models serve as the reference. Recent models are trained on recent text, so a rising overlap with them may mean that writing moved towards the models or that the models
learned from the writing. We therefore compare reference generators by \emph{vintage} on a fixed task: models
whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT, models released in 2023--2025, and models released in 2026.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1)~We define Machine-Style Overlap (\mso{}), a classify-and-count quantity \citep{forman2008} that, for an
optimal classifier, equals the Bayes classifier's false-positive rate on pre-ChatGPT writing; we estimate it with
calibrated classifiers and report how the estimate varies across them (\S\ref{sec:mso}).
(2)~We measure it in arXiv abstracts against 19 reference generators released from 2023 to 2026, including four
whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT and four released in 2026 (\S\S\ref{sec:data}--\ref{sec:method}).
(3)~Abstracts moved far into the LLM region after 2022, more in computer science than in mathematics. Placebo dates and instruments trained on earlier years give gaps at most about a quarter as large (\S\ref{sec:shift}).
(4)~The shift is present, though smaller, against generators that are unlikely to have learned it from
post-ChatGPT writing; overlap with a single generator tends to peak later for more recent generators, so the trajectory depends on the reference's vintage (\S\ref{sec:vintage}).
(5)~Secondary results (\S\S\ref{sec:markers}--\ref{sec:learners}): words mined from each generator rose until 2025 and mostly fell in 2026; stylistic dispersion did not change beyond its trend once length is fixed; parliamentary speech changed little; and, contrary to our plan, English learners' essays are \emph{less} machine-like than their peers'.
Our analysis plan was committed to version control before any result was computed (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}). It stated five hypotheses: pre-ChatGPT \mso{} is non-zero and differs by field (H1); arXiv \mso{} rises above its pre-ChatGPT trend (H2), more than in a less-exposed genre (H3) and more in computer science than in mathematics (H4); and learner essays have higher \mso{} (H5). The vintage analysis was added after simulated review.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Prevalence of LLM-assisted writing.}
Excess-vocabulary methods compare word frequencies with a counterfactual extrapolated from pre-LLM years
\citep{kobak2025, holzwarth2026, lee2026}; distributional methods fit a mixture of human and LLM word
distributions \citep{liang2024monitoring, liang2024mapping, liang2025widespread, geng2024}; classifier- and
detector-based studies label documents \citep{lin2025, brooks2024, sun2025, khan2026}. All target the share of text produced with LLM help; LLM adoption also changes how many papers scientists produce \citep{kusumegi2025}. Closest to ours, \citet{bentley2026} track a composite LLM-associated style index alongside
vocabulary-complexity measures in arXiv abstracts, and \citet{askitas2025} finds an abrupt stylometric shift in economics
working-paper abstracts in 2023 with an event-study design and a classifier. \citet{chang2026} shows that
PubMed, Wikipedia and Stack Exchange text became more predictable to small language models over 2014--2024, a
trend that began before generative AI; our pre-ChatGPT slope points the same way. Our estimand differs: a thresholded overlap (the flag rate of a calibrated classifier) whose pre-ChatGPT level is itself of interest. We also exclude content words from the features, since authors react to publicised markers \citep{geng2025}, and compare reference generators by training-data vintage.

\paragraph{Homogenisation and human adoption.}
Writing or ideating with instruction-tuned models reduces content diversity across users \citep{padmakumar2024, doshi2024, anderson2024homog}, and LLM rewriting compresses stylistic variance \citep{sourati2025}. Preference tuning reduces output diversity \citep{kirk2024, wu2025monoculture} and plausibly produces LLM-overused words
\citep{juzek2025}. LLM-associated vocabulary has grown in human speech \citep{yakura2024, anderson2025spoken},
academic authors adapt their LLM use once overused words are publicised \citep{geng2025}, and adoption of LLM
revision varies across disciplines and author groups \citep{lin2025}. Models trained on their own outputs
degrade when synthetic data replace real data \citep{shumailov2024} but not when the two accumulate
\citep{gerstgrasser2024}. Our vintage comparison asks whether human text still provides a reference independent of LLM style.

\paragraph{Style of human and LLM text.}
LLM text differs systematically from human text in lexico-grammatical features \citep{reinhart2025,
munozortiz2024, berbersardinha2024, herbold2023} and in recurring part-of-speech templates \citep{shaib2024},
but texts cluster by genre more than by source \citep{rallapalli2026}, so any overlap measure must be
genre-specific. Distribution-level comparisons \citep{pillutla2021, yang2026coverage} evaluate models against
human text; we track where human documents fall relative to an LLM region.

\paragraph{Detection and perception.}
Readers misjudge machine text \citep{clark2021, jakesch2023, casal2023, gao2023}; we collect no human judgements. Zero-shot detectors use
token-likelihood statistics \citep{hans2024binoculars, bao2024fastdetectgpt}, and detection needs more text as machine
and human distributions converge \citep{chakraborty2024}; off-the-shelf detectors have flagged non-native writing
\citep{liang2023detectors}, although detectors trained on a large writing assessment showed no such bias \citep{jiang2024} and false-positive
rates on non-native manuscripts vary widely \citep{park2026}. Detectors transfer unevenly to unseen generators
\citep{pu2023zeroshot, li2024mage}, and individual models have distinctive, identifiable output patterns
\citep{sun2025idiosyncrasies}, so a style region depends on the models that define it.

\section{Machine-Style Overlap}
\label{sec:mso}

Fix a genre and a feature map $\phi$ from documents to style vectors. Let $P_{\text{pre}}$ be the distribution of
$\phi(d)$ for human documents written before ChatGPT's release (30 November 2022; ``pre-ChatGPT'' throughout),
and $P_{\text{LLM}}$ the distribution for LLM output in the same genre, an equal-weight mixture over a reference
set of generators and prompting styles chosen by us. For any population $S$ of documents,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MSO}(S) = \Pr_{d\sim S}\big[\, p_{\text{LLM}}(\phi(d)) \ge p_{\text{pre}}(\phi(d)) \,\big].
\end{equation}
With equal priors the Bayes posterior is $\eta(x) = p_{\text{LLM}}(x)/(p_{\text{LLM}}(x)+p_{\text{pre}}(x))$, so
$\mathrm{MSO}(S) = \Pr_S[\eta(\phi(d)) \ge \tfrac12]$; we call $\{x: \eta(x) \ge \tfrac12\}$ the \emph{LLM
region}. \mso{} is the flag rate of the Bayes-optimal classifier, that is, classify-and-count
\citep{forman2008} with a calibrated threshold.

\paragraph{Pre-ChatGPT overlap.}
For $S = P_{\text{pre}}$, \mso{} is the Bayes classifier's false-positive rate, and the Bayes error is
$\tfrac12\big(\mathrm{MSO}(P_{\text{pre}}) + \Pr_{\text{LLM}}[\eta < \tfrac12]\big)$, which is zero only when the
two styles are separable and reaches $\tfrac12$ when they coincide. Prevalence studies that subtract a
pre-period share as misclassification remove the analogue of this quantity under their own feature map.

\paragraph{Overlap after ChatGPT.}
For later populations $S_t$, $\mathrm{MSO}(S_t)$ measures how much writing at time $t$ falls in the region. It says nothing about authorship: a rise is consistent with undisclosed LLM use, with humans adopting LLM style, or both.
If a fraction $\alpha$ of documents were LLM output and the rest unchanged human writing,
$\mathrm{MSO}(S_t) = \mathrm{FPR} + \alpha(\mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR})$, where FPR and TPR are the region's false- and true-positive rates; we use this identity only to translate
\mso{} into an implied $\alpha$ for comparison with prevalence studies.

\paragraph{Estimation.}
We call a trained, calibrated classifier with its 0.5 threshold an \emph{instrument}. A finite instrument only
approximates $\eta$, and its flag rate is neither an upper nor a lower bound on the Bayes quantity. We therefore
report how \mso{} varies across classifier families, held-out generators, generator sets and thresholds;
confidence intervals in the text are document-level bootstrap or regression intervals and exclude this
instrument variation.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{arXiv abstracts.}
We sampled eight arXiv archives by primary category (cs, math, stat, eess, q-bio, econ, astro-ph, cond-mat; ``fields'' below). For each field and each month from January 2015 to September 2026, we fetched through the arXiv API all papers submitted in one randomly placed window of consecutive days and drew up to 200 at random. Field-months with at least 200 papers therefore carry equal weight. Smaller fields contribute fewer documents, especially in early years, so the field mix changes (eess made up \R{robustness_v6.field_share.eess_2015}\% of scored abstracts in 2015 and \R{robustness_v6.field_share.eess_2022}\% in 2022), and the overall series averages a stratified sample and does not represent the arXiv population. The API returns the latest version of each
abstract, so papers first submitted before ChatGPT but revised after it are excluded
(\R{robustness_v4.revision_exclusion_share.y2016}\% of sampled 2016 papers,
\R{robustness_v4.revision_exclusion_share.y2021}\% of 2021 and \R{robustness_v4.revision_exclusion_share.y2022}\% of 2022
papers). Recent papers have had less time to be revised, but scoring only never-revised papers barely changes yearly \mso{} (2024: \R{robustness_v5.never_revised.mso_never_y2024}\% vs \R{robustness_v5.never_revised.mso_all_y2024}\%; 2026: \R{robustness_v5.never_revised.mso_never_y2026}\% vs
\R{robustness_v5.never_revised.mso_all_y2026}\%). After removing abstracts under 50 words,
\R{corpus_stats.arxiv_n_docs} abstracts remain.

\paragraph{Other human text.}
\emph{Parliamentary speech:} Australian House of Representatives Hansard \citep{katz2023scidata,
katz2025hansard}, up to 150 spoken speeches per month from 2015 to July 2025 (\R{corpus_stats.hansard_n_docs}
speeches). \emph{Student essays:} PERSUADE~2.0 \citep{crossley2024persuade}, \R{corpus_stats.essay_n_docs}
pre-ChatGPT essays by US students in grades 6--12 on \R{essay_ell_counts.n_prompts} prompts, with
English-language-learner (ELL) status and a holistic score. In every genre, half of the pre-ChatGPT documents (the
\emph{train pool}) train the instrument and seed LLM prompts; \mso{} is measured on the rest.

\paragraph{Reference generators.}
Table~\ref{tab:gens} lists them. Local models answered three prompt styles, about 150 prompts each:
\emph{P1} write the text from a title or topic; \emph{P2} improve the wording of a human draft (LLM-assisted
revision); \emph{P3} write the text and ``avoid typical AI phrasing''. Prompts were seeded only from train-pool
documents; local models used temperature 0.8 and fixed seeds. The 2026 model (Claude Opus~5.5) answered the same
prompt instances through sub-agent sessions of 50 prompts each, without control over decoding; it is also the
system that drafted this paper (see Acknowledgements). For abstracts we add the RAID generations without adversarial attack
\citep{dugan2024raid}, written from titles of arXiv papers by six 2023 chat models. The main instrument uses
14 generators, each capped at 450 training texts. Five further open models were added after review, for the vintage analysis only: two whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT and three released in 2026. The latter give the 2026 comparison a second source with logged decoding, and each pairs with a 2025 model of the same family. Hansard and essays use the seven local models and the
2026 model.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Cleaning.}
All texts are normalised alike. We remove chat wrappers (``Here's the abstract:''), lines echoing the prompt's title, markdown and trailing notes, and map typographic variants and mis-decoded characters to plain equivalents. LaTeX math becomes a placeholder and LaTeX dashes (\texttt{-{}-}, \texttt{-{}-{}-}) become Unicode dashes: human arXiv abstracts write dashes in LaTeX and chat models in Unicode, a difference that would otherwise separate the sources by encoding alone. Texts are truncated to 250 words.

\paragraph{Features and instruments.}
Features exclude content words to limit topic sensitivity: per-token rates of closed-class words, coarse
part-of-speech trigrams, dependency labels and punctuation marks, plus mean and standard deviation of sentence
length, mean word length and MTLD lexical diversity (measure of textual lexical diversity; \citealp{mccarthy2010}), computed with spaCy~3.8
(\texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm}). Features present in fewer than 1\% of training documents are dropped
(top features in Appendix~\ref{app:features}). The primary instrument is L2-regularised logistic regression
($C{=}0.1$) on standardised features with isotonic calibration; the second uses gradient-boosted trees,
calibrated the same way. Classes are balanced, so a calibrated probability of 0.5 is a likelihood ratio of one.
Cross-validation and calibration folds are grouped so that a revised text and its human source, and all speeches
by one parliamentarian, never fall in different folds. For essays we add two instruments: logistic regression on
sentence embeddings (all-MiniLM-L6-v2), and a one-feature isotonic classifier on mean token negative log-likelihood, the statistic behind perplexity-based detectors, under a small causal language model (Qwen2.5-0.5B, or \gptii{} small, whose training data predate the TOEFL and PERSUADE releases). Both are trained on the
same human and LLM essays as the primary instrument.

\paragraph{Time series.}
We compute monthly \mso{} and fit an interrupted time-series (ITS) model: weighted least squares with a level shift and
a slope change at December 2022 and Newey--West (6 lags) standard errors. The \emph{trend gap} is the fitted
post-ChatGPT value minus the extrapolated pre-ChatGPT trend in June 2025, a month fixed in code before any
time-series result. As robustness checks we vary the pre-trend (quadratic; start in 2017 or 2019; end in 2020),
fit the post-ChatGPT line only up to June 2025, hold the field mix fixed, compute a nonparametric gap (mean observed January--June 2025 value minus the extrapolated trend), and bootstrap
the gap with 12-month residual blocks. Placebo breaks are placed every second month from June 2016 to December
2021, each evaluated 12 months later, and at two fixed dates (November 2019 and 2020) evaluated in June 2022. Drift placebos train the instrument only on human abstracts up to 2016, 2017 or 2018, place a break in the following January and test for a gap in June 2022, 41 to 65 months later and before any LLM writing assistance, so any gap they show reflects instrument ageing.

\paragraph{Vintage.}
To compare vintages on a fixed task, we train one instrument per generator on its title-only (P1) outputs (RAID outputs are title-based). A further instrument pools the generators whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT (the \emph{pre-ChatGPT-data region}): GPT-4 and Llama-2-Chat 70B from RAID, plus Llama-2-Chat~7B and Vicuna~7B run locally on P1. Many RAID outputs summarise the paper (``In the paper titled\dots''); we drop those matching a
summary pattern from this region and from each RAID generator's own region (\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data.summary_register_share}\% of the RAID part of this set; up to \R{arxiv_generator_lengths.single:raid-cohere-chat.summary_share_before_filter}\% for Cohere's model).

\paragraph{Marker words.}
We track two lexical series: the share of abstracts containing a published marker word (\emph{delve},
\emph{underscore}, \emph{showcase}, \emph{meticulous}, \emph{intricate}, \emph{intricacies},
\emph{commendable}, \emph{boast}; \citealp{kobak2025, gray2024, yakura2024}), and, for each reference set, the
share containing one of 20 words mined from that set's own outputs: the words with the highest smoothed log
ratio of document frequency between the outputs and the human abstracts their prompts were built from, so that
most topic words of the prompt set cancel.

\subsection{Separability of human and LLM text}
The instruments separate pre-ChatGPT human text from LLM text well (cross-validated AUC
\R{arxiv_instrument.cv_auc_all} for abstracts, \R{hansard_instrument.cv_auc_all} for speech,
\R{essay_instrument.cv_auc_all} for essays). Generators held out of training are mostly recognised
(arXiv recall from \R{arxiv_lomo.raid-cohere-chat.recall_on_unseen_model}\% for Cohere to
\R{arxiv_lomo.raid-gpt4.recall_on_unseen_model}\% for GPT-4; Table~\ref{tab:lomo} in Appendix~\ref{app:lomo}), but the 2026 model is not:
only \R{arxiv_lomo.claude-opus-5.5.recall_on_unseen_model}\% of its abstracts and
\R{essay_lomo.claude-opus-5.5.recall_on_unseen_model}\% of its essays fall in a region built without it. Its
revisions of human drafts are also hard to separate from human text (AUC
\R{arxiv_separability.claude-opus-5.5/P2.auc}). Lexical diversity is the top LLM-typical feature;
human abstracts use more coordination, adjectival modifiers and longer sentences (Appendix~\ref{app:features}).

\subsection{Pre-ChatGPT overlap varies by field (H1)}
Before ChatGPT, \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% (95\% CI \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.lo}--%
\R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.hi}) of arXiv abstracts fell in the LLM region, as did
\R{hansard_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% (\R{hansard_baseline_pre2022.all.lo}--\R{hansard_baseline_pre2022.all.hi})
of speeches and \R{essay_mso_by_group.all.mso}\% (\R{essay_mso_by_group.all.lo}--\R{essay_mso_by_group.all.hi})
of student essays. The arXiv baseline varies by field (Figure~\ref{fig:fields} in Appendix~\ref{app:fields}; $\chi^2(\R{arxiv_its.baseline_field_heterogeneity.dof})=\R{arxiv_its.baseline_field_heterogeneity.chi2}$,
$p\R{arxiv_its.baseline_field_heterogeneity.p}$), from \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.astro-ph.mso}\% in astrophysics
to \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.econ.mso}\% in economics. Fields also differ in abstract length; reweighting each
field to the overall length distribution changes the values to \R{arxiv_baseline_lenstd.astro-ph.mso_lenstd}\%
and \R{arxiv_baseline_lenstd.econ.mso_lenstd}\%, and abstracts without math keep astrophysics lowest and economics highest. Overall
\mso{} was also rising slowly before ChatGPT (\R{arxiv_its.all.pre_slope_per_year}\pp{} per year).



\subsection{Abstracts moved into the region (H2, H4)}
\label{sec:shift}
Yearly arXiv \mso{} ranged from \R{arxiv_yearly.2016.mso}\% to \R{arxiv_yearly.2020.mso}\% in 2015--2021, was \R{arxiv_yearly.2022.mso}\% in 2022, then \R{arxiv_yearly.2023.mso}\%, \R{arxiv_yearly.2024.mso}\% and \R{arxiv_yearly.2025.mso}\% in 2023--2025 and \R{arxiv_yearly.2026.mso}\% in January--September 2026. The monthly series has been roughly flat since late 2025 (Figure~\ref{fig:ts}a), and its 2026 rise depends on the 2026 model (\S\ref{sec:vintage}). The trend gap in June 2025 is
\R{arxiv_its.all.excess}\pp{} (95\% CI \R{arxiv_its.all.excess_lo}--\R{arxiv_its.all.excess_hi}; block
bootstrap \R{robustness_v4.arxiv_block_bootstrap.lo}--\R{robustness_v4.arxiv_block_bootstrap.hi}), about
\R{robustness_v4.arxiv_pretrend.relative_gap} times the extrapolated trend value. Across the variants in
Table~\ref{tab:robust} that change the threshold, the sample or the trend, the gap stays between
\R{arxiv_its.threshold_06.excess}\pp{} (threshold 0.6) and \R{arxiv_its.no_math.excess}\pp{} (abstracts without math).
It is smaller when the generators that write closest to human abstracts are left out of the region: without the
2026 model (\R{arxiv_gensets_its.no_frontier.excess}\pp) or without revisions of human drafts
(\R{arxiv_vintage.all_no_revision.gap}\pp). The gap does not rest on one feature family. Removing MTLD, the strongest
feature, gives \R{arxiv_ablation.mtld.excess}\pp, and removing all closed-class words, part-of-speech trigrams, dependency
labels, punctuation or sentence statistics gives between \R{arxiv_ablation.function_words.excess} and

Yearly residuals from the pre-ChatGPT trend stay within $\pm$1\pp{} through 2022 and reach \R{arxiv_event.2025.resid}\pp{} in 2025.
\emph{Fields (H4).} The gap is largest in computer science (\R{arxiv_its.cs.excess}\pp) and quantitative

\subsection{Sounding like which AI?}
\label{sec:vintage}
\emph{Generators whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT.} The pre-ChatGPT-data region (GPT-4, Llama-2-Chat 70B,
Llama-2-Chat~7B and Vicuna~7B on the title-only task) reflects models unlikely to have learned their style from post-ChatGPT scientific writing. Their pretraining data predate ChatGPT, and their 2023 instruction tuning used annotator data or shared ChatGPT conversations and, as far as is documented, no recent abstracts.
Abstracts still moved into it: \mso{} was \R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.y2022}\% in 2022,
\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.y2024}\% in 2024 and \R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.y2025}\% in 2025,
and the trend gap is \R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.gap}\pp{} (CI
\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.gap_lo}--\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.gap_hi}), against
\R{arxiv_vintage.local_p1.gap}\pp{} for a title-only region built from the 2024--2025 open models
(Figure~\ref{fig:vintage}a). Its subsets point the same way but differ in size: the RAID models alone give \R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_abstract_register.gap}\pp{} (\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data.gap} with summary-style outputs kept, \R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_plus_chatgpt.gap} with ChatGPT added and summaries kept), and the two small local models, run on exactly our prompts, give \R{arxiv_vintage.old_open_p1.gap}\pp{} (CI \R{arxiv_vintage.old_open_p1.gap_lo}--\R{arxiv_vintage.old_open_p1.gap_hi}). Three quarters of the region's training texts come from RAID, so its generators are not equally weighted and the pooled estimate leans on RAID's prompt. Part of the shift is therefore towards the style
of the pre-ChatGPT-data generators and is unlikely to be an artefact of newer models learning from newer abstracts,
though the estimate from our own prompts is small. Drift placebos for this region (instruments trained on abstracts up to 2016, 2017 or 2018) give
gaps of \R{robustness_v5.pre2022data_drift_to_2016.excess}, $\R{robustness_v5.pre2022data_drift_to_2017.excess}$ and
$\R{robustness_v5.pre2022data_drift_to_2018.excess}$\pp. In 2026 its \mso{} fell back to
\R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.y2026}\%. Because overlap with these generators peaked in 2025, a straight post-ChatGPT line fitted through 2026 understates their June 2025 gap. Measured nonparametrically, the gap is \R{arxiv_vintage.pre2022data_matched.gap_nonparam}\pp{} against \R{arxiv_vintage.local_p1.gap_nonparam}\pp{} for the 2024--2025 open models, and \R{arxiv_vintage.old_open_p1.gap_nonparam}\pp{} for the two small local models.

\emph{One generator at a time.} With the region defined by a single generator on the title-only task, \mso{} rose
from 2022 to 2025 under every generator except Cohere's chat model. Quarter by quarter, overlap with each generator but the 2026 model rose and then fell, and the turn tended to come later for later generators (Figure~\ref{fig:vintage}b). It peaked between 2024Q3 and 2025Q3 under the 2023 models other than Cohere's, in 2025Q2--Q3 under the
2024 models, in 2025Q4 under Gemma~3, Granite~3.3, Qwen3 and Gemma~4, and in 2026Q1--Q2 under gpt-oss, Qwen3.5 and
Granite~4.2; under the 2026 model it was still rising in 2026Q3. From 2026Q2 to 2026Q3 overlap fell under every generator other than the 2026 model, and for gpt-oss and Granite~4.2 that one quarter is the whole decline, so their peaks may mark a shared fluctuation rather than a turn. The rank correlation between release date and
peak quarter is \R{arxiv_vintage_peak.rho} ($n=\R{arxiv_vintage_peak.n}$, $p\R{arxiv_vintage_peak.rho_p}$;
the 2026 model is excluded because its release month is not public). All generators score the same abstracts, so their errors are correlated; a document bootstrap within quarters gives \R{arxiv_vintage_peak_boot.rho_lo}--\R{arxiv_vintage_peak_boot.rho_hi}. The correlation is \R{arxiv_vintage_peak.rho_no2023} without the 2023 models ($n=\R{arxiv_vintage_peak.n_no2023}$) and \R{arxiv_vintage_peak.rho_raid_tied} with all RAID models, whose release months are approximate, dated mid-2023. Individual peak quarters are uncertain by one or two quarters, and by up to five for Cohere's model (Appendix~\ref{app:peaks}). The analysis is exploratory and was chosen after a first test (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}). Gemma~4 (12B released in June 2026) and Granite~4.2 peaked before their release and Qwen3.5 in its release quarter, so authors' use of these models can explain little of the timing. On annual means, \mso{} fell from 2025 to 2026 under \R{arxiv_vintage_summary.n_fell_2026} of the
\R{arxiv_vintage_summary.n_single} generators and rose under gpt-oss
(\R{arxiv_vintage.single:gpt-oss:20b.y2025}\% to \R{arxiv_vintage.single:gpt-oss:20b.y2026}\%), Qwen3.5, Granite~4.2
and the 2026 model (\R{arxiv_vintage.single:claude-opus-5.5.y2025}\% to
\R{arxiv_vintage.single:claude-opus-5.5.y2026}\%). Each change has a document-bootstrap interval that excludes zero
and keeps its sign across five draws of the human training sample and ten refits on 80\% of the generator's texts (Appendix~\ref{app:peaks}), except under Gemma~4
($\R{arxiv_vintage_uncertainty.single:gemma4:12b.change_seed0}$\pp, CI
$\R{arxiv_vintage_uncertainty.single:gemma4:12b.change_lo}$ to \R{arxiv_vintage_uncertainty.single:gemma4:12b.change_hi}).
In all three same-family pairs the newer model's change was higher, for Gemma within Gemma~4's interval. On the \R{arxiv_vintage_peak.n_cutoff} generators with a stated pretraining cutoff, cutoff and release date order the peaks about equally well (rank correlations \R{arxiv_vintage_peak.rho_cutoff} and \R{arxiv_vintage_peak.rho_release_on_cutoff_set}), so we cannot separate the recency of post-training from that of pretraining data. A region pooling
the three 2026 open models, run with logged decoding, also rose (\R{arxiv_vintage.open2026_p1.y2025}\% to
\R{arxiv_vintage.open2026_p1.y2026}\% on annual means), so the late peak does not rest on the uncontrolled generator.

The aggregate 14-generator series (Figure~\ref{fig:ts}a) still rose in 2026, although 12 of its 14 single-generator regions fell. Without the 2026 model it did not (\R{arxiv_gensets.no_frontier.y2025}\% to \R{arxiv_gensets.no_frontier.y2026}\%), and pooled regions need not follow their members' title-only regions: the seven local models pooled over all three prompts rose from \R{arxiv_gensets.local.y2025}\% to \R{arxiv_gensets.local.y2026}\%. If overlap keeps turning down later for later generators, a fixed reference set will eventually show the shift receding,
even if writing keeps moving. Of our comparisons, only the pre-ChatGPT-data region is free of the question of who learned from whom.

of US 8th-grade essays, reproducing the bias reported by \citet{liang2023detectors}, yet it flags the PERSUADE learners less often than their peers, because their writing is less predictable (Appendix~\ref{app:learners}).

\section{Discussion}

Whether abstracts were still moving towards LLM style in 2026 depends on the reference LLM. Overlap tended to peak later for more recent generators, and under three of the four 2026 generators it peaked in
2026 or was still rising, while words mined from most generators fell, so the decline of \emph{delve} need not mean that writing stopped moving. Detectors, prevalence estimators and marker lists built on a
fixed set of models may therefore understate recent change unless the reference is refreshed. Our data cannot say why abstracts in 2026 resemble the newest models. Authors' use of the 2026 open models explains little of their peaks; these models may have learned from recent writing, or they and the authors may share the style of the commercial models authors used in 2024--2026, which, apart from the 2026 model, we did not run. Either way, recent LLMs and recent scientific writing can no longer serve as independent references for each other, a precondition for the recursive dynamics studied in model-collapse work \citep{shumailov2024, gerstgrasser2024} but not evidence of them.

\section{Conclusion}
In arXiv abstracts, Machine-Style Overlap rose from \R{arxiv_baseline_pre2022.all.mso}\% before ChatGPT to \R{arxiv_yearly.2025.mso}\% in 2025, well above its pre-ChatGPT trend, partly towards generators whose pretraining data predate ChatGPT. Overlap with a single generator tended to peak later for more recent generators, so studies of LLM influence on writing should report the vintage of their reference models and update it.

\section*{Limitations}
\mso{} is defined relative to a feature map and a researcher-chosen generator set, and its trajectory after 2025
depends on that set's vintage (\S\ref{sec:vintage}). Four generators are from 2026; one of them was run without
decoding control in batches of 50 prompts and is the system that drafted this paper. The pre-ChatGPT-data region rests
on four generators, three of them built on Llama~2, and their instruction tuning used data from 2023. The vintage comparison holds the task (an abstract from a title) fixed, but not the prompt wording or decoding for RAID, decoding for the 2026 model, or output length. Median lengths range from \R{arxiv_generator_lengths.single:vicuna:7b.median_words} words (Vicuna) to \R{arxiv_generator_lengths.single:raid-llama-chat.median_words}, the truncation length (Llama-2-Chat 70B), within the pre-ChatGPT-data region, from \R{arxiv_generator_lengths.single:raid-mpt-chat.median_words} to \R{arxiv_generator_lengths.single:raid-mistral-chat.median_words} for the other RAID models, and from \R{arxiv_generator_lengths.single:granite3.3:8b.median_words} to \R{arxiv_generator_lengths.single:granite4.2:8b.median_words} for the 2025--2026 open models. The 2026 model's single region is the least distinct from human abstracts: \R{arxiv_vintage.single:claude-opus-5.5.y2015}\% of 2015 abstracts fall in it, against at most \R{arxiv_vintage.single:raid-cohere-chat.y2015}\% for any other generator. The three 2026 open models were added after the pattern of the other generators had been seen, and
single-generator regions rest on 150 texts each (up to 450 for RAID models), so instrument precision differs by vintage. Cross-validation grouped the 2026 model's outputs by source document, not by session, which may flatter its held-in recall. Mined marker lists contain some topic and register words. Post-ChatGPT
``human'' documents may be partly LLM-written, so \mso{} cannot separate LLM use from human adoption of LLM
style, and no causal claim is made. The overall arXiv series averages a stratified sample of eight fields
(two of them in physics). Changes in authorship, subfields and submission volume may contribute despite
stratified sampling and topic and length checks, and excluding papers revised after ChatGPT makes the late
pre-period sample more selected. Confidence intervals ignore instrument variation and clustering by field-month. Classifier families agree on population-level \mso{} for abstracts (\R{arxiv_instrument.mso_logreg}\% vs
\R{arxiv_instrument.mso_gbm}\%) but less on individual documents (Cohen's
$\kappa=\R{arxiv_instrument.kappa_logreg_vs_gbm}$; speech \R{hansard_instrument.kappa_logreg_vs_gbm}, essays
\R{essay_instrument.kappa_logreg_vs_gbm}), and a perplexity instrument reverses its bias between two learner populations. All text is English and truncated to 250 words. Parliamentary speech differs from abstracts in many ways,
and its linear trend fails its own placebo tests. The dispersion measure depends on document length. The learner analysis is
cross-sectional, concerns US school learners, and rests on \R{essay_ell_counts.ell_in_region} in-region learner
essays; its perplexity instruments use two small language models and thresholds calibrated on PERSUADE. The
analysis plan was committed to our own repository and never frozen with a third party.

\section{Parliamentary speech}
\label{app:hansard}
The Hansard trend gap is small and not significant at the primary threshold (\R{hansard_its.all.excess}\pp, CI
$\R{hansard_its.all.excess_lo}$ to \R{hansard_its.all.excess_hi}; Figure~\ref{fig:ts}b), about
\R{robustness_v4.hansard_pretrend.relative_gap} times its extrapolated trend value against
\R{robustness_v4.arxiv_pretrend.relative_gap} for abstracts (\R{robustness_v4.hansard_pretrend.logit_gap}
against \R{robustness_v4.arxiv_pretrend.logit_gap} on the logit scale). With the arXiv region rebuilt from the eight generators used
for Hansard (arXiv gap \R{control_difference.arxiv_excess}\pp), the difference between the genres is
\R{control_difference.difference}\pp{} (CI \R{control_difference.lo}--\R{control_difference.hi}). The comparison is weak, however. Its gap is \R{hansard_its.threshold_06.excess}\pp{} at threshold 0.6 (CI $\R{hansard_its.threshold_06.excess_lo}$ to \R{hansard_its.threshold_06.excess_hi}), and its nonparametric gap ranges from $\R{robustness_v4.hansard_pretrend.linear_to_2020}$ to \R{robustness_v4.hansard_pretrend.quadratic_2015}\pp{} across pre-trend specifications. Of its placebo breaks, \R{robustness_v3.hansard_placebo_grid.share_p_below_05}\% give $p<0.05$, and the 2022 change of government altered the speaker population six months before ChatGPT.
Published marker words also rose in speeches (\R{hansard_its.marker.excess}\pp{} above trend). We read Hansard as
evidence that parliamentary speech changed much less than abstracts.

\section{Dispersion}
\label{app:dispersion}
To connect overlap to homogenisation we measured, for each year, the mean Euclidean distance of abstracts to the
year's centroid in the feature space standardised on pre-ChatGPT abstracts. On all abstracts, dispersion falls
steadily from \R{arxiv_dispersion.2015.dispersion} in 2015 to \R{arxiv_dispersion.2026.dispersion} in 2026, but
abstracts also grew longer, and per-token rates are less noisy in longer texts. With every abstract cut to its first
150 words (abstracts of at least 150 words, 1,200 per year), dispersion declines slowly before ChatGPT
($\R{arxiv_len150_dispersion_trend.gap_2025.trend_per_year}$ per year) and in 2025 lies
\R{arxiv_len150_dispersion_trend.gap_2025.est} above that trend (bootstrap CI
$\R{arxiv_len150_dispersion_trend.gap_2025.ci_lo}$ to \R{arxiv_len150_dispersion_trend.gap_2025.ci_hi}). The interval
excludes an extra decline larger than about three years of the pre-ChatGPT trend, so any faster loss of stylistic
spread is small next to the shift towards the LLM region. Diversity of content or ideas may behave differently \citep{padmakumar2024, doshi2024}.

\section{Learner essays}
\label{app:learners}
Our analysis plan predicted that essays by English-language learners would show \emph{higher} \mso{}. They show
lower: \R{essay_mso_by_group.ELL.mso}\% vs \R{essay_mso_by_group.non-ELL.mso}\%
(\R{essay_ell_counts.ell_in_region} of \R{essay_ell_counts.n_ell} learner essays in the region; odds ratio
adjusted for prompt and grade \R{essay_ell_adjusted.odds_ratio}, CI
\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_lo}--\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_hi}; lower, and significant at $p<0.05$, in all
\R{essay_seed_stability.n_seeds} training draws). Most of the difference is proficiency: adding the holistic
essay score reduces the odds ratio to \R{essay_ell_adjusted.odds_ratio_prof} (CI
\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_lo_prof}--\R{essay_ell_adjusted.or_hi_prof}) and the continuous classifier margin from
$\R{essay_ell_continuous.ell_coef_sd}$ to $\R{essay_ell_continuous.ell_coef_sd_prof}$ standard deviations (CI
$\R{essay_ell_continuous.coef_lo_prof}$ to $\R{essay_ell_continuous.coef_hi_prof}$). In essays the LLM region is
largely a region of fluent, lexically diverse writing. The other instruments agree in direction (Figure~\ref{fig:learners}). Sentence embeddings flag \R{essay_embedding_instrument.ELL.mso}\% of learner and \R{essay_embedding_instrument.non-ELL.mso}\% of other essays. Perplexity under \gptii{} separates human from LLM essays less well than style features (AUC \R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.heldin_auc} against \R{essay_instrument.cv_auc_all}), so it flags \R{essay_perplexity_instrument_gpt2.all.mso}\% of all human essays. It flags \R{essay_perplexity_instrument_gpt2.ELL.mso}\% of learner vs \R{essay_perplexity_instrument_gpt2.non-ELL.mso}\% of other essays (odds ratio \R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.odds_ratio}; \R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.odds_ratio_prof}, CI \R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.or_lo_prof}--\R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.or_hi_prof}, after adjusting for proficiency), because these learners' essays are \emph{less} predictable (mean token log-perplexity
\R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.mean_logppl_ell} vs \R{essay_perplexity_adjusted_gpt2.mean_logppl_nonell}).
The same \gptii{} instrument reproduces the bias reported by \citet{liang2023detectors} on their data: it flags
\R{essay_liang_perplexity_gpt2.Real TOEFL.mso}\% of TOEFL essays by non-native test-takers, which are highly
predictable (\R{essay_liang_perplexity_gpt2.Real TOEFL.mean_logppl}), but
\R{essay_liang_perplexity_gpt2.Real US 8th grade.mso}\% of US 8th-grade essays
(\R{essay_liang_perplexity_gpt2.Real US 8th grade.mean_logppl}); Qwen2.5-0.5B gives the same pattern
(\R{essay_liang_perplexity.Real TOEFL.mso}\% vs \R{essay_liang_perplexity.Real US 8th grade.mso}\%). \gptii{}'s training data predate the public release of the TOEFL essays (and PERSUADE), so that contrast is unlikely to come from memorised test essays, although the 8th-grade essays derive from data public since 2012. In these data a perplexity instrument penalises predictable writing. The TOEFL essays are predictable and the PERSUADE learners' essays are not, so which learners such a detector flags depends on the population, consistent with
detectors that showed no bias in a large writing assessment \citep{jiang2024}. The TOEFL and 8th-grade sets also
differ in genre, prompt, age and length.
