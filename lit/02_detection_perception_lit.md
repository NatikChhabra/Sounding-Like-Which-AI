# Lit survey 02: Human & automated detection of AI text; perception of "AI style"; AI in peer review

Compiled 2026-10-12. Every entry below was located via web search / fetch of the publisher, ACL Anthology, PMLR, arXiv or journal page during this session. Numbers are reported only where they appeared in the source abstract/text (or in a search excerpt quoting it). Status tags:

- **VERIFIED**: title, authors, venue, year and URL/DOI confirmed; numbers taken from abstract/text.
- **PARTIAL**: paper confirmed, but a specific number or the venue came only from a secondary source; flagged inline.
- **UNVERIFIED**: could not confirm from a primary source; do not cite without checking.

## 1. Human ability to detect AI-generated text

### 1.1 Clark et al. 2021: "All That's 'Human' Is Not Gold: Evaluating Human Evaluation of Generated Text" (VERIFIED)
Elizabeth Clark, Tal August, Sofia Serrano, Nikita Haduong, Suchin Gururangan, Noah A. Smith. ACL-IJCNLP 2021 (Long), pp. 7282-7296. Outstanding Paper. DOI 10.18653/v1/2021.acl-long.565. https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.565 (arXiv 2107.00061)
- Untrained non-expert evaluators judged human vs. GPT-2/GPT-3 text in stories, news and recipes. Accuracy on GPT-3 text was **at chance**. Three quick training methods raised accuracy to **at most 55%**, and the gain was not significant across domains. Evaluators gave contradictory reasons for their judgments.
- Relevance: this is the standard early evidence that lay readers cannot tell the two apart, and that the reasons they give for their judgments are unreliable. It predates ChatGPT.

### 1.2 Jakesch, Hancock & Naaman 2023: "Human heuristics for AI-generated language are flawed" (VERIFIED)
PNAS 120(11): e2208839120 (March 2023). DOI 10.1073/pnas.2208839120. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10089155 (arXiv 2206.07271)
- Six experiments, **N = 4,600**. Participants could not reliably tell AI-written from human-written self-presentations in professional, hospitality and dating contexts. Their judgments rested on intuitive but flawed heuristics: first-person pronouns, contractions and family topics were read as "human". AI could exploit these cues to produce text perceived as "more human than human."
- Relevance: central evidence for the claim that perceived "humanness" rests on surface cues that overlap between the two sources and can be gamed.

### 1.3 Dugan et al. 2023: "Real or Fake Text?: Investigating Human Ability to Detect Boundaries Between Human-Written and Machine-Generated Text" (RoFT) (VERIFIED)
Liam Dugan, Daphne Ippolito, Arun Kirubarajan, Sherry Shi, Chris Callison-Burch. AAAI 2023, 37(11): 12763-12771. DOI 10.1609/aaai.v37i11.26501. https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/26501 (arXiv 2212.12672)
- Annotators had to find the sentence where a human-written passage switched to LM continuation. The task was hard and skill varied widely across annotators, but people could improve with incentives. Genre affected which errors models made, and certain sentence-level features predicted which sentences annotators flagged. Released more than 21,000 annotations.
- Relevance: mixed or co-authored text is the realistic case, and the human-to-AI boundary is hard to locate.

### 1.4 Casal & Kessler 2023: "Can linguists distinguish between ChatGPT/AI and human writing?: A study of research ethics and academic publishing" (VERIFIED)
J. Elliott Casal, Matt Kessler. Research Methods in Applied Linguistics 2(3): 100068. DOI 10.1016/j.rmal.2023.100068.
- **72 linguist reviewers** (from top applied-linguistics journals) judged AI vs. human research abstracts. Overall **positive identification rate was 38.9%**. Each reviewer saw 4 samples: none got all 4 right and **13% got all 4 wrong**. Their stated rationales were linguistically plausible but inaccurate. A survey of 27 editors found divided views on ethical AI use.
- Relevance: domain experts in language did worse than chance on short academic genres. This is the strongest "experts can't tell" citation for a CL paper.

### 1.5 Gao et al. 2023: "Comparing scientific abstracts generated by ChatGPT to real abstracts with detectors and blinded human reviewers" (VERIFIED)
Catherine A. Gao et al. npj Digital Medicine 6:75 (Apr 26, 2023). DOI 10.1038/s41746-023-00819-6. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10133283/
- Blinded human reviewers correctly identified **68%** of ChatGPT-generated abstracts and **misidentified 14% of real abstracts as generated**. Reviewers said the task was surprisingly difficult; suspected abstracts were "vaguer and more formulaic." The GPT-2 Output Detector reached AUROC 0.94 (ChatGPT-3.5 era).
- Relevance: even domain scientists produce false accusations against real human writing (14%), and the cue they report ("formulaic") also describes much human academic prose.

### 1.6 Russell, Karpinska & Iyyer 2025: "People who frequently use ChatGPT for writing tasks are accurate and robust detectors of AI-generated text" (VERIFIED)
Jenna Russell, Marzena Karpinska, Mohit Iyyer. ACL 2025 (Long), pp. 5342-5373. DOI 10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.267. https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267 (arXiv 2501.15654)
- Annotators labelled 300 non-fiction articles (GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, o1-Pro vs. human). Those who **frequently use LLMs for writing** detected AI text well without training. A **majority vote of 5 such "experts" misclassified only 1 of 300** articles, beating most commercial and open-source detectors even after paraphrasing or "humanization." The experts relied on "AI vocabulary" but also on formality, originality and clarity.
- Relevance: **important counter-evidence.** Detection is possible for a small expert group under majority vote. Frame the thin-line argument as "for typical readers and single judges" rather than "for everyone." (The abstract gives no figure for non-expert accuracy; check the paper body before citing one.)

### 1.7 Jones & Bergen 2024: "Does GPT-4 pass the Turing test?" (VERIFIED)
Cameron R. Jones, Benjamin K. Bergen. NAACL 2024 (Long), pp. 5183-5210. DOI 10.18653/v1/2024.naacl-long.290. https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.290
- Online Turing test. The best GPT-4 prompt passed in **49.7%** of games, compared with ELIZA 22%, GPT-3.5 20% and humans 66%. Judges decided mainly on **linguistic style (35%)** and socio-emotional traits (27%). Participants who knew more about LLMs, or had played more games, detected AI better.
- Relevance: style is the main basis people use to judge humanness. Note that the arXiv v1 numbers differ; cite the NAACL version.

### 1.8 Jones & Bergen 2024: "People cannot distinguish GPT-4 from a human in a Turing test" (PARTIAL: venue)
arXiv 2405.08007 (May 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.08007
- Randomized, preregistered 2-player Turing test. GPT-4 was judged human **54%** of the time, compared with ELIZA 22% and real humans 67%.
- Venue: confirmed only as an arXiv preprint. One aggregator lists FAccT '25, which I could not confirm, so cite it as arXiv.

### 1.9 Jones & Bergen 2025: "Large Language Models Pass the Turing Test" (VERIFIED as preprint)
arXiv 2503.23674 (Mar 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23674
- Preregistered 3-party Turing tests, 5-minute chats. **GPT-4.5 with a humanlike persona prompt was picked as the human 73% of the time**, significantly more often than the real human. LLaMa-3.1-405B with the persona prompt reached 56% (not significantly different from humans). GPT-4o reached 21% and ELIZA 23%.
- Relevance: the strongest "more human than human" behavioural result. The effect depends on the persona prompt.

### 1.10 Fleckenstein et al. 2024: "Do teachers spot AI? Evaluating the detectability of AI-generated texts among student essays" (VERIFIED; per-group % PARTIAL)
Johanna Fleckenstein, Jennifer Meyer, Thorben Jansen, Olaf Köller, Stefan D. Keller, Jens Möller. Computers & Education: Artificial Intelligence 6: 100209 (2024). DOI 10.1016/j.caeai.2024.100209. Open access: https://hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de/bitstreams/3a70a194-ae71-4ccb-ba3c-7442d4968af2/download
- Two experiments with **89 novice and 200 experienced teachers**. Neither group could reliably identify ChatGPT essays among student essays, and **both were overconfident**.
- The figures 45.1% (pre-service) and 37.8% (experienced) correct on AI texts come only from a vendor blog (Originality.ai). **Verify in the PDF before citing.**

### 1.11 Scarfe et al. 2024: "A real-world test of artificial intelligence infiltration of a university examinations system: A 'Turing Test' case study" (VERIFIED)
Peter Scarfe, Kelly Watcham, Alasdair Clarke, Etienne Roesch. PLOS ONE 19(6): e0305354. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0305354. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11206930
- In a blind field test, fully AI-written (GPT-4) exam answers were submitted into 5 undergraduate psychology modules at a UK university. **94% went undetected** by markers. AI submissions scored on average about half a grade boundary higher than real students, and there was an 83.4% chance that a set of AI submissions would outperform a random set of real ones.
- Relevance: ecologically valid evidence with real markers who did not know about the study.

### 1.12 Porter & Machery 2024: "AI-generated poetry is indistinguishable from human-written poetry and is rated more favorably" (VERIFIED)
Brian Porter, Edouard Machery. Scientific Reports 14: 26133. DOI 10.1038/s41598-024-76900-1. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11564748
- **N = 1,634** non-expert participants scored **46.6% accuracy** (below chance) and were *more* likely to judge AI poems as human-authored than the real poems by canonical poets. A second sample (N = 696) rated AI poems higher on rhythm and beauty. The authors attribute this to shared but flawed heuristics.
- Caveat: participants were non-experts and the model was ChatGPT-3.5.

### 1.13 Köbis & Mossink 2021: "Artificial intelligence versus Maya Angelou: Experimental evidence that people cannot differentiate AI-generated from human-written poetry" (VERIFIED)
Nils Köbis, Luca D. Mossink. Computers in Human Behavior 114: 106553. DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2020.106553. (arXiv 2005.09980)
- With GPT-2 poems, people could not reliably tell machine from human poems. Participants showed a slight aversion to algorithm-generated poetry whether or not its origin was disclosed. (Sample sizes not checked.)

### 1.14 Kalashnikova et al. 2026: "TURING: Evaluating Human Abilities to Identify AI-Generated Texts" (VERIFIED)
Natalia Kalashnikova, Nicolas De Bufala, Sophie Fayad, Laurent Cervoni. LREC 2026, pp. 4527-4535. https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.355/
- 9,164 annotations from **214 participants** on 500 texts (balanced). After quality filtering, human accuracy was **about 59%**, with a slight bias toward labelling texts as human. Accuracy depended on genre and on the generating LLM, and learning over the task was negligible. "Monotonous" or incohesive text was associated with AI labels. Two automated tools reached about 88% on this dataset.
- Relevance: recent large-sample evidence that typical humans are only modestly above chance.

### 1.15 Milička et al. 2025: "Humans can learn to detect AI-generated texts, or at least learn when they can't" (VERIFIED as preprint; journal version PARTIAL)
Jiří Milička, Anna Marklová, Ondřej Drobil, Eva Pospíšilová. arXiv 2505.01877 (May 2025). The arXiv page links DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0333007 (PLOS ONE; journal page not checked).
- **254 Czech native speakers** judged GPT-4o vs. human text pairs. Without feedback, participants made the **most errors when they were most confident**. Immediate feedback improved both accuracy and calibration. Participants initially believed AI text was stylistically rigid, which was a misconception. (The abstract gives no accuracy percentages.)

### 1.16 Wang et al. 2025/2026: "Is Human-Like Text Liked by Humans? Multilingual Human Detection and Preference Against AI" (VERIFIED as arXiv; venue per arXiv comment)
Yuxia Wang, Rui Xing, Jonibek Mansurov, et al. (26 authors). arXiv 2502.11614; arXiv comment says "ACL 2026 Main". https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11614
- **19 annotators** across 16 datasets, 9 languages and 9 domains averaged **87.6% accuracy**. Remaining gaps were concreteness, cultural nuance and diversity, and prompting to address these partly closed them in over 50% of cases. Humans did not always *prefer* human text, especially when they could not tell the source.
- Relevance: **counter-evidence** (trained annotators doing well), but the finding that prompting closes the gaps supports the "convergence" argument.

### 1.17 Fiedler & Döpke 2025, lecturers and German theses (UNVERIFIED)
Seen only in a search snippet: "63 lecturers ... only half of AI-generated texts recognized". Full citation not located, so do not cite without finding the primary source.

## 2. Automated detection: unreliability, attacks, bias, theory

### 2.1 Sadasivan et al.: "Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?" (VERIFIED)
Vinu Sankar Sadasivan, Aounon Kumar, Sriram Balasubramanian, Wenxiao Wang, Soheil Feizi. arXiv 2303.11156 (v1 Mar 2023; v4 Jan 2025); the arXiv record says it was published in TMLR. https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156
- (Recursive) paraphrasing attacks break watermarking, neural and zero-shot detectors. Theoretically, as an LM's distribution approaches human text, **even the best possible detector performs only marginally better than random** (an AUROC bound in terms of total variation distance).
- Relevance: the core "impossibility-flavoured" result. Pair it with 2.10 for the counter-position.

### 2.2 Krishna et al. 2023: "Paraphrasing evades detectors of AI-generated text, but retrieval is an effective defense" (VERIFIED)
Kalpesh Krishna, Yixiao Song, Marzena Karpinska, John Wieting, Mohit Iyyer. NeurIPS 2023. https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/575c450013d0e99e4b0ecf82bd1afaa4-Abstract-Conference.html (arXiv 2303.13408)
- An 11B paraphraser (DIPPER) evaded watermarking, GPTZero, DetectGPT and OpenAI's classifier. For example, **DetectGPT accuracy fell from 70.3% to 4.6% at 1% FPR**. Retrieval over the provider's own generations detected 80-97% of paraphrases at 1% FPR, but this works only for the API provider.

### 2.3 Liang et al. 2023: "GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers" (VERIFIED)
Weixin Liang, Mert Yuksekgonul, Yining Mao, Eric Wu, James Zou. Patterns 4(7): 100779 (2023). DOI 10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779. (arXiv 2304.02819)
- Seven widely used detectors misclassified TOEFL essays by Chinese students as AI-generated with an **average false-positive rate of 61.3%**, while US 8th-grade essays were classified accurately. Simple prompting both reduced the bias and enabled evasion. The authors caution against using detectors in evaluative or educational settings.
- (The figure that one detector flagged 97.8% comes from a secondary source only; check it in the paper.)
- Relevance: low lexical diversity and constrained style are read as "AI". **Human writing that resembles AI style gets flagged**, which shows the overlap directly.

### 2.4 Dugan et al. 2024: "RAID: A Shared Benchmark for Robust Evaluation of Machine-Generated Text Detectors" (VERIFIED)
Liam Dugan et al. ACL 2024 (Long), pp. 12463-12492. DOI 10.18653/v1/2024.acl-long.674. https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.674
- More than **6M generations** across 11 models, 8 domains, 11 adversarial attacks and 4 decoding strategies. All 12 detectors evaluated (8 open, 4 closed) are **easily fooled** by adversarial attacks, sampling changes, repetition penalties and unseen models.

### 2.5 Kirchenbauer et al. 2023: "A Watermark for Large Language Models" (VERIFIED)
John Kirchenbauer, Jonas Geiping, Yuxin Wen, Jonathan Katz, Ian Miers, Tom Goldstein. ICML 2023, PMLR 202:17061-17084. Outstanding Paper. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/kirchenbauer23a.html
- Green-list token watermark detected with a statistical test. It requires the generating provider's cooperation and does not help with text from non-watermarking models.

### 2.6 Zhang et al. 2024: "Watermarks in the Sand: Impossibility of Strong Watermarking for Language Models" (VERIFIED)
Hanlin Zhang, Benjamin L. Edelman, Danilo Francati, Daniele Venturi, Giuseppe Ateniese, Boaz Barak. ICML 2024, PMLR 235:58851-58880. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/zhang24o.html (arXiv 2311.04378)
- Under natural assumptions, **strong watermarking is impossible**. A generic attack using quality and perturbation oracles removed the watermarks of Kirchenbauer et al., Kuditipudi et al. and Zhao et al. with only minor quality loss.

### 2.7 Mitchell et al. 2023: "DetectGPT: Zero-Shot Machine-Generated Text Detection using Probability Curvature" (VERIFIED)
Eric Mitchell, Yoonho Lee, Alexander Khazatsky, Christopher D. Manning, Chelsea Finn. ICML 2023, PMLR 202:24950-24962. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/mitchell23a.html
- Uses log-probability curvature under perturbation. AUROC rose from 0.81 (strongest zero-shot baseline) to 0.95 on GPT-NeoX-20B fake news. It fails under paraphrasing (see 2.2).

### 2.8 Bao et al. 2024: "Fast-DetectGPT: Efficient Zero-Shot Detection of Machine-Generated Text via Conditional Probability Curvature" (VERIFIED)
Guangsheng Bao, Yanbin Zhao, Zhiyang Teng, Linyi Yang, Yue Zhang. ICLR 2024. https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/6b8c6f846c3575e1d1ad496abea28826-Abstract-Conference.html
- Roughly 75% relative improvement over DetectGPT and about 340x faster.

### 2.9 Hans et al. 2024: "Spotting LLMs With Binoculars: Zero-Shot Detection of Machine-Generated Text" (VERIFIED)
Abhimanyu Hans et al. ICML 2024, PMLR 235:17519-17537. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/hans24a.html
- Reports detecting **more than 90% of ChatGPT samples at 0.01% FPR** with no training.
- Relevance: strong in-distribution numbers. The thin-line argument must engage with this, but RAID (2.4) shows that such detectors degrade under attacks and domain shift.

### 2.10 Verma et al. 2024: "Ghostbuster: Detecting Text Ghostwritten by Large Language Models" (VERIFIED)
Vivek Verma, Eve Fleisig, Nicholas Tomlin, Dan Klein. NAACL 2024, pp. 1702-1717. DOI 10.18653/v1/2024.naacl-long.95. https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.95
- Feature search over weaker-LM probabilities reached **99.0 F1** across domains. It also released essay, creative-writing and news benchmarks.

### 2.11 Chakraborty et al. 2024: "Position: On the Possibilities of AI-Generated Text Detection" (VERIFIED)
Souradip Chakraborty, Amrit Singh Bedi, Sicheng Zhu, Bang An, Dinesh Manocha, Furong Huang. ICML 2024, PMLR 235. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/chakraborty24a.html (arXiv 2304.04736)
- **Counter to 2.1.** Detection remains possible unless the human and machine distributions coincide over their whole support, but the required sample size (number of samples or text length) grows as the distributions converge.
- Relevance: frames the issue precisely. As styles converge, detection needs more text, so it becomes unreliable at the level of a single short document.

### 2.12 Weber-Wulff et al. 2023: "Testing of detection tools for AI-generated text" (VERIFIED; detailed numbers PARTIAL)
Debora Weber-Wulff, Alla Anohina-Naumeca, Sonja Bjelobaba, Tomáš Foltýnek, Jean Guerrero-Dib, Olumide Popoola, Petr Šigut, Lorna Waddington. International Journal for Educational Integrity 19: 26. DOI 10.1007/s40979-023-00146-z.
- Covered 14 tools, including Turnitin and PlagiarismCheck. The authors conclude they are "neither accurate nor reliable", biased toward labelling text as human, and degraded further by obfuscation and machine translation. (The figures "all <80% accuracy" and "only 5 >70%" are from secondary sources; verify before citing.)

### 2.13 Education false accusations: no peer-reviewed primary study located (UNVERIFIED)
Found only journalism: a Bloomberg test of 500 pre-ChatGPT essays, and Common Sense Media 2024 reporting false-positive rates of 20% for Black, 10% for Latino and 7% for White students. These are not peer-reviewed and were not verified, so cite Liang et al. 2023 (2.3) and Gao et al. (1.5, 14% false accusations) instead.

## 3. Perception of "AI-sounding" style, AI tells, disclosure effects, "AI slop"

### 3.1 Kobak et al. 2025: "Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary" (VERIFIED)
Dmitry Kobak, Rita González-Márquez, Emőke-Ágnes Horvát, Jan Lause. Science Advances 11(27): eadt3813 (2025). DOI 10.1126/sciadv.adt3813. (arXiv 2406.07016)
- An excess-word analysis of **15.1M PubMed abstracts** found that at least **13.5% of 2024 abstracts** were LLM-processed (a lower bound, reaching 40% in some subcorpora). Style words such as "delves", "showcasing" and "underscores" surged.
- Relevance: this is the empirical source of the "delve" tell, and it also documents style diffusion into the human-authored literature.

### 3.2 Czuma 2026: "Em-ergence of the em-dash: a population-level rise in em-dash frequency in medRxiv preprints at the dawn of the large-language-model era" (VERIFIED as preprint)
Przemysław Czuma. arXiv 2606.29540 (June 2026), preregistered on OSF, not peer-reviewed.
- Analysed N = 69,632 medRxiv Discussion sections. Em-dash prevalence rose from **4.23% pre-ChatGPT to 11.58% after**, reaching 8.0% in 2024 and 20.3% in 2025 (OR 2.96). The author stresses that it is a **population-level indicator, not a per-paper detector**.
- Relevance: the em-dash "tell" has an empirical basis at population level but is invalid for judging individual documents.

### 3.3 Reinhart et al. 2025: "Do LLMs write like humans? Variation in grammatical and rhetorical styles" (VERIFIED)
Alex Reinhart, Ben Markey, Michael Laudenbach, Kachatad Pantusen, Ronald Yurko, Gordon Weinberg, David West Brown. PNAS 122(8): e2422455122. DOI 10.1073/pnas.2422455122. (arXiv 2410.16107)
- Parallel human/LLM corpora (Llama 3, GPT-4o) analysed with Biber features showed **systematic differences**. These persist with model scale and are larger for instruction-tuned models than for base models.
- Relevance: **important counter-evidence** that LLM style is measurably distinct at register level. The paper should argue that the line is thin for readers and single documents, while acknowledging that aggregate stylometry can separate the two.
- (Author list confirmed on arXiv 2410.16107.)

### 3.4 Altay & Gilardi 2024: "People are skeptical of headlines labeled as AI-generated, even if true or human-made, because they assume full AI automation" (VERIFIED)
Sacha Altay, Fabrizio Gilardi. PNAS Nexus 3(10): pgae403. DOI 10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae403.
- Two preregistered experiments, **N = 4,976** (US/UK). An "AI-generated" label lowered perceived accuracy and sharing intention **regardless of whether the headline was true, or was actually written by a human**. The penalty was about one third the size of a "false" label, and it stems from assuming full automation.
- Relevance: the *belief* that text is AI-written changes how it is judged, independent of content. This supports the claim that the AI/human label carries social weight that the text itself does not justify.

### 3.5 Wang, Sturgis & de Kadt 2025: "AI labeling reduces the perceived accuracy of online content but has limited broader effects" (VERIFIED as preprint)
arXiv 2506.16202 (v2 Feb 2026). https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.16202
- Nationally representative probability sample, **n = 3,861**. An AI label reduced the perceived accuracy of a policy news article and interest in the policy, but had no effect on policy support.

### 3.6 Akpinar et al. 2026: "LLM or Human? Perceptions of Trust and Information Quality in Research Summaries" (VERIFIED as preprint)
Nil-Jana Akpinar, Sandeep Avula, CJ Lee, Brandon Dang, Kaza Razat, Vanessa Murdock. arXiv 2601.15556 (Jan 2026).
- **ML-expert readers struggled to identify LLM-written abstracts**, yet their *assumptions* about LLM involvement shaped quality and trust ratings. **LLM-edited abstracts were rated higher** than fully human or fully LLM abstracts. (Numbers not extracted.)
- Relevance: directly on point for a CL paper. Perception of AI involvement, not actual involvement, drives judgments, and hybrid text scores best.

### 3.7 Shaib, Chakrabarty, Garcia-Olano & Wallace 2025: "Measuring AI 'Slop' in Text" (VERIFIED as preprint)
arXiv 2509.19163 (v2 Jan 2026). https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19163
- Builds a taxonomy of "slop" from expert interviews (NLP, writing, philosophy) with interpretable dimensions. Binary slop judgments are "somewhat subjective" but correlate with coherence and relevance.
- Relevance: the main academic citation for the term "AI slop" in text.
- (Kommers et al., "Why Slop Matters", was seen only in a secondary report and is UNVERIFIED.)

### 3.8 Studies of human writing being flagged as "AI-sounding" by *people*
No controlled study located that measures readers labelling human text *because of* em dashes or "delve" (searched). The closest verified evidence is Gao et al. (1.5, 14% of real abstracts judged AI), Casal & Kessler (1.4, 13% got every item wrong), Jakesch et al. (1.2, flawed heuristics), Porter & Machery (1.12, human poems judged AI), and Kalashnikova et al. (1.14, "monotonous" text labelled AI). **Gap: a citable opportunity, but the authors cannot fill it without human subjects.**

## 4. Peer review and AI-written papers

### 4.1 Bianchi, Queen, Thakkar, Sun & Zou 2025: "Exploring the use of AI authors and reviewers at Agents4Science" (VERIFIED; read full PDF)
arXiv 2511.15534 (Nov 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15534
- **315 submissions**: 62 were desk-rejected, and 253 were reviewed by 3 LLM reviewers (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4). The top 79 also received human expert review, and **48 were accepted**. All accepted papers listed an AI as first author.
- LLM reviewers had a mean pairwise Pearson correlation of 0.48. Mean scores were GPT-5 2.30 (harshest), Claude 3.0 and Gemini 4.23. Mean absolute difference from human scores was GPT-5 0.91, Claude 1.09 and Gemini 2.73.
- **About 44% of submissions had no hallucinated references**, so the majority had at least one flagged reference. Two papers attempted prompt injection against the LLM reviewers. Other issues: sycophancy in LLM reviews, and outputs that were "technically correct" but seen by humans as lacking creativity. Accepted papers had more human involvement.

### 4.2 Yamada et al. 2025: "The AI Scientist-v2: Workshop-Level Automated Scientific Discovery via Agentic Tree Search" (VERIFIED as preprint)
Yutaro Yamada, Robert Tjarko Lange, Cong Lu, Shengran Hu, Chris Lu, Jakob Foerster, Jeff Clune, David Ha. arXiv 2504.08066 (Apr 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08066. Blog: https://sakana.ai/ai-scientist-first-publication/
- Of 3 fully AI-generated submissions to an ICLR 2025 workshop (reported as ICBINB, "I Can't Believe It's Not Better"), one, "Compositional Regularization: Unexpected Obstacles in Enhancing Neural Network Generalization", **scored 6.33 (6/7/6) and exceeded the acceptance threshold**. Reviewers knew some submissions might be AI-generated but not which ones. The paper was **withdrawn by prior agreement**. Sakana concedes none of the three met its own bar for the main conference.
- Follow-up: Sakana's site reports a *Nature* paper (March 2026), "The AI Scientist: Towards Fully Automated AI Research". The URL suggests DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10265-5, but the Nature page was not fetched, so treat it as **PARTIAL**. Per Sakana, it reports that an automated reviewer "matches human performance" on conference decisions, and lists limitations including hallucinated citations.

### 4.3 Liang et al. 2024: "Monitoring AI-Modified Content at Scale: A Case Study on the Impact of ChatGPT on AI Conference Peer Reviews" (VERIFIED)
Weixin Liang et al. ICML 2024 (oral), PMLR 235:29575-29620. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/liang24b.html (arXiv 2403.07183)
- Corpus-level maximum-likelihood estimation found that **6.5-16.9% of peer-review text** at ICLR 2024, NeurIPS 2023, CoRL 2023 and EMNLP 2023 was substantially LLM-modified. The share was higher in low-confidence reviews, those submitted near the deadline, and those from reviewers who did not engage with rebuttals. No comparable shift was found in Nature-family journals.
- Relevance: methodologically important. Reliable estimation is possible **at corpus level, not per document**.

### 4.4 Latona et al. 2024/2025: "The AI Review Lottery: Widespread AI-Assisted Peer Reviews Boost Paper Scores and Acceptance Rates" (VERIFIED)
Giuseppe Russo Latona, Manoel Horta Ribeiro, Tim R. Davidson, Veniamin Veselovsky, Robert West. arXiv 2405.02150; published in Proc. ACM on Human-Computer Interaction (CSCW), Oct 2025 (per EPFL/Princeton records).
- Using GPTZero, **at least 15.8% of ICLR 2024 reviews** were AI-assisted. AI-assisted reviews scored higher in 53.4% of discordant pairs. Borderline papers with an AI-assisted review were **4.9 percentage points more likely to be accepted**.

### 4.5 Liang et al. 2024: "Mapping the Increasing Use of LLMs in Scientific Papers" (VERIFIED)
Weixin Liang, Yaohui Zhang, Zhengxuan Wu, et al., James Zou. COLM 2024. (arXiv 2404.01268)
- 950,965 papers (2020 to Feb 2024). Estimated LLM-modified sentences reached **up to 17.5% in computer science** and up to 6.3% in mathematics and Nature-portfolio papers. A later Nature Human Behaviour version (DOI 10.1038/s41562-025-02273-8, per search, **PARTIAL**) reports up to 22% for CS through Sept 2024.
