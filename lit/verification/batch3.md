## herbold2023

**Citing sentence (l.102-103):** "LLM text differs systematically from human text in lexico-grammatical features \citep{reinhart2025, munozortiz2024, berbersardinha2024, herbold2023}".

**Source:** Herbold, Hautli-Janisz, Heuer, Kikteva, Trautsch, "A large-scale comparison of human-written versus ChatGPT-generated essays", *Scientific Reports* 13, 18617 (2023), doi:10.1038/s41598-023-45644-9 (PMC10616290). Preprint arXiv:2304.14276 (titled "AI, write an essay for me: ...").

**Evidence:** The study compares argumentative essays by students with essays generated by ChatGPT. Expert teachers rated the essays, and the authors analysed linguistic features. ChatGPT essays have "more nominalizations" and "greater lexical diversity", plus fewer discourse and epistemic markers. Those are lexico-grammatical differences.

**Verdict:** SUPPORTED.

**Bib:** Title, authors, year and journal are correct. Volume 13, number 1. The article number is missing: add `pages={18617}`.

## holzwarth2026

**Citing sentence (l.83-84):** "Excess-vocabulary methods compare word frequencies with a counterfactual extrapolated from pre-LLM years \citep{kobak2025, holzwarth2026, lee2026}".

**Source:** arXiv:2608.10715 (v1, 11 Aug 2026), Holzwarth, González-Márquez, Kobak, "Most biomedical publications show signs of LLM-assisted writing".

**Evidence:** The method estimates LLM use from word frequencies. For each marker word, the authors fit a linear regression to 2018-2022 frequencies and extrapolate it to obtain the expected human frequency. In their words, they "fit a linear regression to the five-year period (2018–2022)".

**Verdict:** SUPPORTED.

**Bib:** Title, authors (3), year and eprint match. No journal-ref, so it is an arXiv preprint only.

## jakesch2023

**Citing sentences:**
- l.48: "readers are poor at telling human from machine text \citep{clark2021, jakesch2023, casal2023}"
- l.110-111: "people rely on flawed cues \citep{jakesch2023}"

**Source:** Jakesch, Hancock, Naaman, "Human heuristics for AI-generated language are flawed", *PNAS* 120(11), e2208839120 (2023), doi:10.1073/pnas.2208839120.

**Evidence:** Six experiments with 4,600 participants. Participants could not detect AI-generated self-presentations in professional, hospitality and dating contexts. Their judgments relied on "intuitive but flawed heuristics" such as first-person pronouns, contractions and family topics.

**Verdict:** SUPPORTED for both sentences. Note that the text studied is short self-presentations (profiles), not general prose. That is fine for the general claim on l.48.

**Bib:** Correct. Optionally add `pages={e2208839120}`.

## juzek2025

**Citing sentences:**
- l.95-97: "preference tuning reduces output diversity \citep{kirk2024, wu2025monoculture} and plausibly produces LLM-overused words \citep{juzek2025}"
- l.356-357: "chat tuning moves them much further in, in line with reports that preference tuning narrows output style \citep{kirk2024, juzek2025}"

**Source:** Juzek & Ward, "Why Does ChatGPT "Delve" So Much? Exploring the Sources of Lexical Overrepresentation in Large Language Models", arXiv:2412.11385 (16 Dec 2024). Published at COLING 2025.

**Evidence:** The paper identifies 21 focal words whose rise is likely due to LLM use. It finds no evidence that architecture, algorithms or training data cause the overuse. Model comparisons are "consistent with RLHF playing a role", and an exploratory human study gives mixed results.

**Verdict:**
- l.96-97: SUPPORTED. "Plausibly produces" matches the paper's hedged conclusion.
- l.357: PARTIALLY SUPPORTED. Juzek & Ward do not report that preference tuning narrows output *style*. They report that RLHF may contribute to the overuse of specific *words*.

**Bib fix:** The entry says `year = {2024}` (@misc arXiv), but the key says 2025, so the paper will render "Juzek and Ward, 2024". Replace it with the peer-reviewed version:

  author = {Juzek, Tom S. and Ward, Zina B.},

## katz2025hansard

**Citing sentence (l.160-161):** "The Australian Hansard database \citep{katz2025hansard}, restricted to spoken speeches (no interjections or written questions), up to 150 per month from January 2015 to July 2025".

**Source:** Zenodo doi:10.5281/zenodo.17351233. Katz, Lindsay; Alexander, Rohan, "A new, comprehensive database of all proceedings of the Australian Parliamentary Debates (1998-2025)", v8, published 14 Oct 2025, CC BY 4.0.

**Evidence:** The database covers each House of Representatives sitting day from 2 March 1998 to 31 July 2025. The end date matches the paper's "to July 2025". The documented schema (GitHub README `col_types`) has `interject`, `q_in_writing`, `question`, `answer` and `fedchamb_flag` flags. The filter the paper describes is therefore possible with this data.

**Verdict:** PARTIALLY SUPPORTED. The data, years and filter are correct. However, the database covers only the **House of Representatives**, not the Senate, and "The Australian Hansard database" implies both chambers.

**Proposed wording:** "\emph{Parliamentary speech.} The Australian House of Representatives Hansard database \citep{katz2025hansard}, restricted to ..." (The caption or elsewhere could also say "Australian House of Representatives speech".)

**Bib:** Title, creators, year and publisher are correct. Optional: the peer-reviewed description of the database is Katz & Alexander, "Digitization of the Australian Parliamentary Debates, 1998–2022", *Scientific Data* 10, 567 (2023), doi:10.1038/s41597-023-02464-w. Citing it next to the Zenodo record is good practice.

## khan2025

**Citing sentence (l.86):** "classifier- and detector-based studies label documents \citep{lin2025, brooks2024, sun2025, khan2025}. All target the share of text produced with LLM help."

**Source:** arXiv:2509.08306 (v1, 10 Sep 2025), Khan, Ibrahim, Aldahoul, Rahwan, Zaki, "Who Gets Seen in the Age of AI? Adoption Patterns of Large Language Models in Scholarly Writing and Citation Outcomes".

**Evidence:** The study covers about 230,000 Scopus computer-science articles from 2021-2025. It applies the Binoculars zero-shot detector to each article. The detector gives a continuous score and a binary human/AI label (F1-optimised threshold), and the authors use it as a proxy for LLM adoption by region. The main research question is citation outcomes, but the adoption measurement rests on per-document detector labels.

**Verdict:** SUPPORTED.

**Bib:** Correct. arXiv only, with no journal-ref.

## kirk2024

- l.357: "in line with reports that preference tuning narrows output style \citep{kirk2024, juzek2025}"

**Source:** Kirk, Mediratta, Nalmpantis, Luketina, Hambro, Grefenstette, Raileanu, "Understanding the Effects of RLHF on LLM Generalisation and Diversity", arXiv:2310.06452 (v1 Oct 2023, v3 Feb 2024). Published at ICLR 2024.

**Evidence:** "RLHF significantly reduces output diversity compared to SFT across a variety of measures". The paper tests summarisation and instruction following on two base models.

**Citing sentences:**
- l.44-47: "Corpus studies find clear traces of LLM-assisted writing in scientific abstracts, peer reviews, press releases and encyclopedia articles \citep{kobak2025, ...}"

**Source:** Kobak, González-Márquez, Horvát, Lause, "Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary", *Science Advances* 11(27), 2 Jul 2025, doi:10.1126/sciadv.adt3813 (arXiv:2406.07016, whose journal-ref confirms this).

**Evidence:**
- The paper analyses over 15M PubMed abstracts from 2010-2024 and estimates that at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were LLM-processed.
- The counterfactual is "linear extrapolation of word frequencies in 2021 and 2022".
- **Marker words confirmed:** the Results section reads "delves (r=28.0), underscores (r=13.8), and showcasing (r=10.7)". "Intricate", "commendable" and "meticulously" also appear (Fig. S6 list, related-work text).
- "Boast" does **not** appear in Kobak et al. It has to be supported by gray2024 or yakura2024, which are outside this batch. Please check that one of them lists it.

**Verdict:** SUPPORTED for all three uses.

**Bib:** Correct (authors, title, journal, volume 11, number 27, 2025, DOI).

## lee2026

**Citing sentence (l.83-84):** excess-vocabulary methods compare word frequencies with a counterfactual extrapolated from pre-LLM years.

**Source:** arXiv:2609.07447 (v1, 7 Sep 2026), Aron Lee (single author, INTFRAME Research), "An LLM-Associated Register Shift in Korean Journal Abstracts: A Morphology-Aware Excess-Vocabulary Study, 2018-2026".

**Evidence:** The paper adapts the excess-vocabulary method to Korean morphology using 398,296 KCI abstracts. Excess is defined as frequency above the pre-2023 trend.

**Verdict:** SUPPORTED.

**Bib:** Title, author, year and eprint match. Caveat: the arXiv page calls it a working paper (not peer reviewed, one author, independent affiliation). It is fine as one of three citations for the method, but do not lean on it alone.
